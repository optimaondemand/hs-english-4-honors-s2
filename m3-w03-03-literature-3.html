Sacrifice or Erasure?
Grete’s verdict, Gregor’s death, and the spring outing
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
After the lodgers give notice, Grete argues that the family must be rid of the creature and that it cannot be Gregor.
Gregor goes back to his room and dies before morning. The lesson reads his end and the family’s outing, and keeps three readings open: self-sacrifice, self-erasure, or both.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can summarize Grete’s argument and the way her word for Gregor changes.
2
I can explain what Gregor’s last thoughts add to the question of sacrifice.
3
I can set out the evidence for self-sacrifice, for self-erasure, and for both.
📋 READING
▶
Reread Part III from the lodgers’ notice to the end of the novella.
📚 PART 1 · TAKING THE NAME AWAY

Once the lodgers have given notice, Grete addresses her parents and strikes the table to gain their attention. She says she will not call the creature her brother, and that the family must try to be rid of it. She argues that they have done all that could be asked of them. When her father wonders aloud whether Gregor might understand, she answers that he cannot, and then she goes further: the family must stop believing that the creature is Gregor.

Her reasoning is this. A real Gregor, she says, would have recognized long before that people cannot live with such an animal, and he would have left voluntarily. The family would have lost a brother but could have gone on and remembered him with respect. The proof she offers is circular: the creature’s continued presence shows that it is not Gregor. Within the speech she moves from naming him to calling him it, and the father agrees with her.

⚡ FIRST READ
Grete argues that if the creature were Gregor, he would have gone away on his own. What does the argument do?
📚 PART 2 · GREGOR'S LAST HOURS

Gregor hears all of this. With great effort he turns himself around, and no one hurries him. He crawls back toward his room, and the family says nothing. The mother has fallen asleep in her chair. Grete has been standing ready, and as soon as he is inside she springs forward, shuts the door and locks it, calling out to her parents in relief.

Gregor can no longer move, and the pain is fading. His thoughts turn to his family, with tenderness and love. He feels that he must disappear, even more firmly than his sister feels it. He remains in this state until the clock strikes three, and he dies as the light begins to show at the window.

In the morning the charwoman finds him, prods him with a broom, and announces that he is dead. The parents and Grete come out. The father gives thanks to God, and all four cross themselves. The lodgers, who had given notice, are ordered out and go meekly. The family writes letters of excuse to their employers and takes a tram into the open country in warm sunshine. They talk over their prospects and find them better than expected. The parents, looking at Grete, notice that she has grown into a lovely young woman and think it is time to find her a husband. The novella ends as Grete stretches her body.

The story makes one more formal move. After Gregor dies, the narration, which stayed close to him throughout, follows the family out of the flat.

📚 PART 3 · THREE READINGS

Critics have argued for a century over how to take this ending. The text supports more than one reading, and each has to be weighed against the others.

⚖ THREE READINGS OF GREGOR'S END
ReadingWhat supports itWhat strains it
Self-sacrificeHis last thoughts are of his family, with love. He feels that he must go. He dies without protest and leaves them free.He does not choose to die. He is locked in, starving, and wounded, and the story never shows him deciding.
Self-erasureThe family has taken his name, his room, and his place. He comes to share their verdict on himself, and his disappearance does exactly what Grete asked.The text gives him tenderness at the end, not self-contempt.
BothHis love is real, and so is the family’s use of it: a gift that was also demanded, and accepted without acknowledgment.Holding both requires saying which details carry each reading.
⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. What is circular about Grete’s reasoning?
2. A reader argues that Gregor’s death is self-sacrifice. Which detail best supports that reading?
3. Why does the ending resist a single reading?
🔑 KEY TERMS
self-sacrifice: giving up one’s life or interests for the sake of others.
self-erasure: the removal of one’s own presence, name, or claim.
circular argument: an argument whose evidence already assumes its conclusion.
