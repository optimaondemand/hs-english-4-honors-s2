📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The rescued hobbits are given a house, a meal, and a night’s rest, and nothing is asked in return.
Chapter 7 is a chapter of hospitality freely given. It also contains the oddest test of the Ring in the book: a being who handles it without effect. The lesson sets the welcome against the way hospitality works elsewhere and asks what Tom’s immunity suggests about desire and power.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe the hospitality Tom and Goldberry offer and what they do not ask.
2
I can explain what the Ring does and does not do when Tom handles it.
3
I can draw a careful inference about desire and power from the evidence, without claiming more than the text says.
📋 READING
▶
Read “In the House of Tom Bombadil”, Book I, Chapter 7, in your copy.
▶
Look again at the farewell in Chapter 8, “Fog on the Barrow-downs”, for Tom’s advice and the limit of his country.
📚 PART 1 · HOSPITALITY FREELY GIVEN

The welcome is complete. Goldberry greets the four hobbits at the door, tells them to fear nothing, and shuts out the night. Tom arrives, offers water to wash and soft slippers, and sets a supper of cream, honeycomb, bread and butter. The beds are deep, and Goldberry wishes each guest a night of peace, with nothing passing door or window but moonlight, starlight and the wind off the hill. In the morning there is breakfast, and then a day of rain, tales and rest.

Tom asks for nothing. His questions are many, and he draws out more of Frodo’s story than Frodo has told even Gandalf, but he states no price and expects none. When Frodo asks whether Tom heard the cry for help, Tom says that he did not, and that he was busy singing. He was there by an errand of his own, gathering water-lilies for Goldberry. His words are worth keeping: Just chance brought me then, if chance you call it. (Book I, Chapter 7) Gandalf had suggested in Chapter 2 that the finding of the Ring was no accident. Tom’s remark carries the same doubt about what chance is.

⚡ FIRST READ
What does Tom’s hospitality ask of the hobbits in return?
📚 PART 2 · THE RING AND THE MASTER

Frodo asks Goldberry who Tom is, and her answer is short: he is. She calls him the Master of that country and denies that this means ownership. Asked whether all the land belongs to him, she says no, and adds in a low voice: That would indeed be a burden (Book I, Chapter 7). The principle she states in its place is this: The trees and the grasses and all things growing or living in the land belong each to themselves. (Book I, Chapter 7)

The evening brings a test. Tom asks to see the Ring, and Frodo hands it over without knowing why. Tom looks through it, puts it on the end of his finger, and does not vanish. He spins it in the air, and it disappears with a flash, and he hands it back with a smile. Frodo, a little annoyed that Tom treats lightly what Gandalf treated as perilous, slips the Ring on to check that it is the real one. Merry cannot see him. Tom can, and says so: Take off your golden ring! (Book I, Chapter 7).

Tom BombadilPuts it on and does not vanish; spins it so that it disappears; returns it without a sign of wanting it.Seems untouched. The text does not say why.

What the scene shows is narrow and worth stating narrowly. The Ring has no visible hold on Tom, and it does not make him disappear. It does not follow that the text explains him. Goldberry’s remarks suggest a connection: a Master who does not own the things he is master of may have little in him for the Ring to pull on. The other holders so far all wanted something from it, and Tom is the one who does not seem to. That is an inference, and the text does not state it.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Goldberry says that owning the land would be a burden. What does this suggest about Tom’s mastery?
2. Tom puts the Ring on and does not vanish. What does the scene most reasonably suggest?
3. Frodo wears the Ring and Merry cannot see him, but Tom can. What does this show?
📚 PART 3 · THE LIMITS OF THE WELCOME, AND A LOOK BACK AT THE LODGERS

Tom’s hospitality has an edge. He gives the hobbits advice for the next day: go north over the western slopes, keep to the green grass, and avoid old stone and the dead in their mounds unless they have hearts that never falter. He teaches them a rhyme to sing in danger. And he is clear about the limit of his reach. When he sees them off, he explains that he cannot always be near: Tom can't be always near to open doors and willow-cracks. (Book I, Chapter 8)

The contrast with the Samsa household is useful. In Part III of The Metamorphosis, the household receives three paying lodgers with every courtesy while the son is kept out of sight. The welcome there is conditional on payment, and the person with the best claim to belong is the one hidden away. Tom’s house reverses each term: the guests pay nothing, the shelter goes to the lost and the frightened, and no one in the house is kept out of sight. Hospitality in this chapter is a gift, and it comes with a stated boundary.

🔑 KEY TERMS
hospitality: kindness shown to guests and strangers, especially food and shelter.
immunity: freedom from a power that affects others.
inference: a conclusion drawn from evidence that the text does not state outright.
master: a person with authority or skill; Goldberry separates it from owning.
