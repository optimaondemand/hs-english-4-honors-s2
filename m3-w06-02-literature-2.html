📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Marcus argues that a person is made for work and for other people.
Three passages carry the argument: the morning passage, on why he rises; the passage on difficult people, which ends with hands, feet, and eyelids; and a short image of a limb lying by itself. Together they place Marcus’s self inside a larger body.

📋 READING
▶
Read the three passages below from Meditations (Books V, I, and VIII). Full text: Gutenberg eBook #2680.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
The Stoics held that the universe is ordered by reason and that each kind of thing has a nature and a task. A human being’s task, in this view, is rational action in the service of other people. Marcus applies the idea to himself on the day’s first hour, when the work of ruling an empire is waiting.
📚 PART 1 · WHY RISE

The morning passage is an argument with himself. Marcus meets his own reluctance with questions about what a human being is for. He asks whether he was made for a warm bed and for pleasure, or for “a man’s work.” He then points to plants, sparrows, ants, spiders, and bees, each of which does its work without complaint.

“In the morning when thou findest thyself unwilling to rise, consider with thyself presently, it is to go about a man’s work that I am stirred up. Am I then yet unwilling to go about that, for which I myself was born and brought forth into this world? Or was I made for this, to lay me down, and make much of myself in a warm bed? ... And was it then for this that thou wert born, that thou mightest enjoy pleasure? Was it not in very truth for this, that thou mightest always be busy and in action? ... And wilt not thou do that, which belongs unto a man to do? Wilt not thou run to do that, which thy nature doth require? ... and shall actions tending to the common good of human society, seem more vile unto thee, or worthy of less respect and intention?”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book V

Marcus grants that rest is natural and that nature sets it a limit. What he rejects is a life organized around comfort. The work he names at the end is specific: “actions tending to the common good of human society.” The question of what a person is for is answered by what a person is part of.

📚 PART 2 · FELLOW-WORKERS

Marcus forecasts the day’s difficult people: the idle, the thankless, the false, the unsociable. His answer treats them as ignorant, not as enemies. They act as they do “through ignorance of that which is truly good and truly bad,” and the wrongdoer is a kinsman “by participation of the same reason.”

“Betimes in the morning say to thyself, This day ... with an idle curious man, with an unthankful man, a railer, a crafty, false, or an envious man; an unsociable uncharitable man. ... All these ill qualities have happened unto them, through ignorance of that which is truly good and truly bad. ... But I that understand the nature of that which is good, that it only is to be desired, and of that which is bad, that it only is truly odious and shameful: who know moreover, that this transgressor, whosoever he be, is my kinsman, not by the same blood and seed, but by participation of the same reason, and of the same divine particle; How can I either be hurt by any of those, since it is not in their power to make me incur anything that is truly reproachful? or angry, and ill affected towards him, who by nature is so near unto me? for we are all born to be fellow-workers, as the feet, the hands, and the eyelids; as the rows of the upper and under teeth: for such therefore to be in opposition, is against nature; and what is it to chafe at, and to be averse from, but to be in opposition?”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book I, section XV, in this translation (most modern editions number it Book II, section 1)

The image that closes the passage is the one the week is named for. People are born to be fellow-workers, like the feet, the hands, and the eyelids, and like the rows of upper and lower teeth. Each of these works with its pair, so opposition between them is “against nature.” The argument is practical as well as moral: a person cannot be harmed by what is not in his power to make shameful, and anger at a kinsman is a fault in the body, not a defense of it.

📚 PART 3 · THE PART CUT OFF

A shorter image, in Book VIII, shows the same idea from the other side. A hand, a foot, or a head lying by itself is the picture of a person who is offended by what happens or who acts against what Marcus calls “mutual correspondence, and society among men.”

“If ever thou sawest either a hand, or a foot, or a head lying by itself, in some place or other, as cut off from the rest of the body, such must thou conceive him to make himself, as much as in him lieth, that either is offended with anything that is happened, (whatsoever it be) and as it were divides himself from it: or that commits anything against the natural law of mutual correspondence, and society among men: or, he that, commits any act of uncharitableness. Whosoever thou art, thou art such, thou art cast forth I know not whither out of the general unity, which is according to nature. ... However, herein is matter of joy and exultation, that thou mayst be united again.”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book VIII

Two points follow. Marcus places the cutting in the person: the one who is offended or uncharitable divides himself from the whole. And he adds a way back, since such a person “mayst be united again.”

The comparison with Gregor reverses the image. In Marcus the person cuts himself off by anger or unkindness. In The Metamorphosis the household carries out the separation: Gregor is shut away, and the family’s care shrinks until it stops. Emerson’s nonconformist also stands apart, but on principle, in refusal of a crowd’s opinion. Marcus counts standing apart from other people as against nature, and warns against anger and offended withdrawal. The two thinkers do not describe the same separation.

🔑 KEY TERMS
fellow-worker: a person who works with others toward a shared end.
common good: the good of the whole society, as against the good of one member.
kinsman: a relative; here a person tied to Marcus by shared reason.
