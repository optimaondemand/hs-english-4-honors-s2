📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A pronoun stands in for a noun, and the reader must be able to tell which noun without guessing.
The noun a pronoun refers to is its antecedent. The three chapters this week have many characters sharing a scene, so they offer clear examples of pronouns that work and of pronouns that would fail in a careless sentence.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can name the antecedent of a pronoun.
2
I can identify an ambiguous, vague, or missing pronoun reference.
3
I can revise a sentence so that each pronoun has one clear antecedent.
4
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · THE SKILL: PRONOUN REFERENCE

A pronoun takes the place of a noun that has already been named. That noun is the antecedent. In Sam hauled Frodo out, and Frodo coughed, the second Frodo could be replaced by he only if the reader still knows who he is. When two nouns could be the antecedent, the reference is ambiguous. When a pronoun such as this, that, it, or which points at a whole idea and not a noun, it is vague. When no antecedent has been named at all, as in they say, it is missing.

Three repairs are available. Repeat the noun. Rewrite the sentence so that only one noun can be meant. Or turn the sentence into a direct quotation, which lets the speaker use I. Context can also make a reference clear, as in the last two examples on the board.

✎ ON THE BOARD · PRONOUNS THAT WORK AND PRONOUNS THAT DO NOT
When Frodo met Tom, he laughed.
Ambiguous. He could be Frodo or Tom. Repair: When Frodo met Tom, Tom laughed.
Merry told Pippin that he had lost the path.
Ambiguous. Repair: Merry told Pippin, “I have lost the path.”
The hobbits fell asleep under the willow. This worried Sam.
Vague. This points at an event, not a noun. Repair: Their sudden sleepiness worried Sam.
They say the trees of the Forest move.
Missing antecedent. Repair: Merry says the trees of the Forest move.
Sam gripped him by the jacket, and dragged him from under the root; and then with difficulty hauled him on to the bank. (Book I, Chapter 6)
Clear. Him is Frodo, the only person in the water, and the verbs show who acts and who is acted on.
Merry was looking from side to side, and seemed already uncertain which way to go. Pippin noticed it. (Book I, Chapter 6)
Clear by context. It points back to Merry’s uncertainty, named in the sentence before, and no other noun competes.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from Chapters 6 to 8. Each entry gives the sense Tolkien’s narrator uses.

enmity (noun) · deep ill will; the state of being an enemy
Root: Latin inimicus, enemy, from in- (not) + amicus (friend).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 6
furtive (adjective) · done in secret; sly or stealthy
Root: Latin furtivus, stolen, from fur (thief).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 6
usurper (noun) · one who seizes a place or power that belongs to another
Root: Latin usurpare, to take into use, to seize for oneself; related to usus (use).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 7
perilous (adjective) · full of danger
Root: Latin periculum, trial or danger; the chapter uses the adverb perilously.
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 7
incantation (noun) · words chanted as a spell
Root: Latin incantare, to chant a spell over, from in- (upon) + cantare (to sing).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 8
disquieting (adjective) · causing unease or worry
Root: dis- (undo) + quiet, from Latin quies (rest).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 8
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH

English borrowed this French phrase as a farewell wish to someone about to travel.

bon voyage (French: “good journey”)
Tom’s parting words at the border of his country were a kind of bon voyage, given in rhyme and with a warning attached.
🔑 KEY TERMS
antecedent: the noun a pronoun stands for.
ambiguous reference: a pronoun that could refer to more than one noun.
vague reference: a pronoun such as this or which that points at an idea and not a noun.
missing antecedent: a pronoun with no noun in the sentence or the one before.
