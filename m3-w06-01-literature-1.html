Week 6
What Is in Our Power
Marcus Aurelius on what belongs to a person and what does not
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Marcus sorts the world into what is in a person’s power and what is not.
The Meditations are private notes, and the same sorting recurs through them. The body, the course of a life, and other people’s praise lie outside a person’s power. Judgment and action lie inside it. The retreat Marcus recommends is a retreat into that inner part.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can state the line Marcus draws between what is and is not in a person’s power.
2
I can explain what Marcus means by retiring into oneself and what he says must already be there.
3
I can compare Marcus’s inner retreat with Gregor’s confinement in his room.
📋 READING
▶
Read the three passages below from Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (Books V, VI, and IV). Full text: Gutenberg eBook #2680.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Marcus Aurelius (121 to 180) ruled the Roman Empire from 161 until his death and followed the Stoic school of philosophy. Stoicism treats the control of one’s own judgments as the center of a good life. The Meditations are notes he wrote to himself, some of them on military campaign, and were not written for publication. The English on this page follows Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation, which uses forms such as thou, thee, and thyself.
📚 PART 1 · THE LINE

Marcus asks what could hold a person in the world once the world is seen as unstable. His answer is a short list of duties: worship the gods, do good to other people, bear with them, and do them no wrong. He then sets a limit on what a person can claim as his own.

“What is it then that doth keep thee here, if things sensible be so mutable and unsettled? and the senses so obscure, and so fallible? and our souls nothing but an exhalation of blood? and to be in credit among such, be but vanity? What is it that thou dost stay for? an extinction, or a translation; either of them with a propitious and contented mind. But still that time come, what will content thee? what else, but to worship and praise the Gods; and to do good unto men. To bear with them, and to forbear to do them any wrong. And for all external things belonging either to this thy wretched body, or life, to remember that they are neither thine, nor in thy power.”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book V

The list of duties is addressed to the person. The next sentence names what is not the person’s: “all external things belonging either to this thy wretched body, or life,” which are “neither thine, nor in thy power.” The word wretched marks how little weight Marcus gives the body beside conduct.

⚡ FIRST READ
Which item on Marcus’s outside-the-power list is hardest to accept as outside a person’s power?
📚 PART 2 · WHAT IS IN A PERSON’S POWER

A second passage sorts people by where they place their happiness. The ambitious place it in another person’s praise, the pleasure-seeker in sensation, and the wise person in “his own action.” It then makes a claim about opinion.

“The ambitious supposeth another man’s act, praise and applause, to be his own happiness; the voluptuous his own sense and feeling; but he that is wise, his own action. XLVII. It is in thy power absolutely to exclude all manner of conceit and opinion, as concerning this matter; and by the same means, to exclude all grief and sorrow from thy soul. For as for the things and objects themselves, they of themselves have no such power, whereby to beget and force upon us any opinion at all.”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book VI

The claim is narrow. Events and objects “have no such power” to force an opinion on a person. What happens is one thing; the judgment passed on it is another, and only the second is the person’s own. Marcus draws the conclusion that grief can be excluded by excluding the opinion, which treats grief as something a judgment adds to an event.

📚 PART 3 · THE RETREAT WITHIN

People imagine peace as a place, Marcus says: “country villages, the sea-shore, mountains.” He calls that wish “simplicity in the highest degree,” because the retreat that matters is always available.

“They seek for themselves private retiring places, as country villages, the sea-shore, mountains; yea thou thyself art wont to long much after such places. But all this thou must know proceeds from simplicity in the highest degree. At what time soever thou wilt, it is in thy power to retire into thyself, and to be at rest, and free from all businesses. A man cannot any whither retire better than to his own soul; he especially who is beforehand provided of such things within, which whensoever he doth withdraw himself to look in, may presently afford unto him perfect ease and tranquillity. By tranquillity I understand a decent orderly disposition and carriage, free from all confusion and tumultuousness. Afford then thyself this retiring continually, and thereby refresh and renew thyself.”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book IV

One condition runs through the passage. The retreat helps only the person “beforehand provided of such things within.” The inner place is not empty; it holds what the person has already thought, chosen, and valued. Marcus defines the result, tranquillity, as “a decent orderly disposition and carriage,” and ends with an instruction to repeat the retreat “continually.”

Gregor Samsa also ends up alone in a room, but the comparison turns on who controls the door. Marcus’s retreat can be entered at will: “At what time soever thou wilt.” Gregor’s room is closed on him by the household, and the story shows what he has stored inside him only through his behavior toward them. The retreat Marcus describes is a resource a person carries; the room in the story is a space others assign.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Marcus says the wish for a retreat in the country “proceeds from simplicity in the highest degree.” What is he arguing?
2. Marcus says objects and events have “no such power” to force an opinion on a person. What follows from that claim?
3. How does Gregor’s room differ from the retreat Marcus describes?
🔑 KEY TERMS
Stoic: a follower of the school that treats judgment and action, not circumstance, as a person’s own.
external things: in Marcus’s usage, the body, reputation, and the course of a life.
tranquillity: calm; an orderly state of mind and bearing.
retreat: a withdrawal from other people or events, here into the soul.
