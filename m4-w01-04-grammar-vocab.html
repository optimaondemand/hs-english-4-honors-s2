📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
An appositive is a noun or noun phrase that sits beside another noun and renames it.
Tolkien’s narrator uses appositives to introduce people and things quickly, and a writer analyzing the book can use them to fold evidence into a sentence without a pause.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can find an appositive and the noun it renames.
2
I can punctuate an appositive as essential (no commas) or nonessential (commas).
3
I can combine two short sentences by turning one into an appositive.
4
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · THE SKILL: APPOSITIVES

An appositive renames the noun next to it. In Frodo, Bilbo’s heir, lives at Bag End, the phrase Bilbo’s heir is a second noun phrase for Frodo. A nonessential appositive adds information about a noun that is already identified, so commas set it off on both sides. An essential appositive is needed to say which one is meant, so there are no commas: Frodo’s friend Merry keeps the door.

A quick test: remove the appositive. If the sentence still names exactly the same person or thing, the appositive was nonessential and takes commas. If the sentence now points at the wrong person, or at several, the appositive was essential.

✎ ON THE BOARD · TWO KINDS OF APPOSITIVE
Gandalf, a wizard known in the Shire for fireworks, brings a cart of rockets to Bag End.
Nonessential. Gandalf is already identified, so commas set off a wizard known in the Shire for fireworks.
Sam Gamgee, the gardener’s youngest son, hears the whole plan under the window.
Nonessential. The gardener’s youngest son renames Sam Gamgee.
The Ring, a plain band of gold, shows its letters only in fire.
Nonessential. A plain band of gold renames the Ring.
Frodo’s friend Merry keeps watch over the hall.
Essential. Frodo has several friends, so Merry says which one. No commas.
composed by Bilbo himself, the first Hobbit to become famous in the world at large, (Prologue)
From the Prologue. The phrase after the comma renames Bilbo.
the job was mainly carried on by his youngest son, Sam Gamgee. (Book I, Chapter 1)
From Chapter 1. Sam Gamgee renames his youngest son.

Appositives also combine sentences. Gollum lived beneath the mountains. Gollum was once called Sméagol. becomes Gollum, once called Sméagol, lived beneath the mountains. The second sentence has become an appositive, and the sentence is shorter and tighter.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from the Prologue and the first two chapters. Each entry gives the sense Tolkien’s narrator uses.

unobtrusive (adjective) · not drawing attention; modest and quiet
Root: Latin obtrudere, to thrust upon, from ob- (against) + trudere (push); un- reverses it.
Where it appears: Prologue, “Concerning Hobbits”
perpetual (adjective) · lasting without end or change
Root: Latin perpetuus, continuous, from per- (through) + petere (to go toward).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 1
inexhaustible (adjective) · impossible to use up
Root: in- (not) + Latin exhaurire, to draw out, from ex- + haurire (to draw).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 1
ominous (adjective) · suggesting that something bad is coming
Root: Latin omen, a sign of what will happen.
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 2
dominion (noun) · rule or control over a people or place
Root: Latin dominium, ownership, from dominus (master of a house).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 2
malice (noun) · the wish to do harm to someone
Root: Latin malitia, badness, from malus (bad).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 2
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN

English kept this Latin phrase to name the existing condition of things, especially when someone proposes to change it.

status quo (Latin: “the state in which”)
The Shire’s status quo depended on guardians that its hobbits never saw.
🔑 KEY TERMS
appositive: a noun or noun phrase that renames the noun beside it.
nonessential: adding detail to a noun already identified; set off by commas.
essential: needed to say which one is meant; no commas.
noun phrase: a noun with its modifiers, which can act as a single noun in a sentence.
