Apostrophe, Personification, and Extended Metaphor
Grammar + Vocab · Week 7
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Each of the three poems speaks to something that cannot answer, gives life to what has none, or builds one comparison across a poem.
Blake speaks to a tiger, Herbert to his own heart, and Dickinson turns hope into a bird. The three figures are apostrophe, personification, and extended metaphor. They often work together, and each can be found by asking a short question.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can name apostrophe, personification, and extended metaphor and say what each does.
2
I can find each figure in lines from the three poems.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · THREE FIGURES

Apostrophe is address to a person who is absent or dead, or to something nonhuman or abstract, as if it could hear. Ask: who is being spoken to, and can it answer? Personification gives human or living traits to an idea, object, or animal. Ask: what is acting, and is it something that could not act? Extended metaphor is a single comparison carried across several lines or a whole poem. Ask: do later details stay inside the same image?

The figures overlap. Herbert’s speaker addresses his heart and treats it almost as another person, which joins apostrophe to personification. Blake’s tiger is addressed (apostrophe) and its making is described as smithing (extended metaphor).

✎ ON THE BOARD · SIX PASSAGES FROM THE POEMS
Tiger, tiger, burning bright
In the forests of the night,
Apostrophe. The speaker addresses an animal that cannot answer.
Not so, my heart: but there is fruit,
And thou hast hands.
Apostrophe. The speaker addresses his own heart as if it were a second person.
When the stars threw down their spears,
And watered heaven with their tears,
Personification. Stars act as soldiers and mourners, which are human roles.
Before my sighs did drie it: there was corn
Before my tears did drown it.
Personification. Sighs and tears act on the wine and the corn as agents.
Hope is the thing with feathers
That perches in the soul,
Extended metaphor. Hope is a bird, and the comparison runs on through sings, gale, little bird, and crumb.
What the hammer? what the chain?
In what furnace was thy brain?
Extended metaphor. The tiger’s making is a smith’s work, and the stanza stays inside that image.

📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from the three poems. Each entry gives the sense the poem uses.

symmetry (noun) · balanced, matching proportion
Root: Greek symmetria (agreement in measure), from syn- (together) + metron (measure)
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?
sinews (noun) · tendons; figuratively, sources of strength
Root: Old English sinu (tendon)
Could twist the sinews of thy heart?
aspire (verb) · to rise toward; to strive for something high
Root: Latin aspirare (to breathe upon, to strive toward), from ad- + spirare (to breathe)
On what wings dare he aspire?
abash (verb) · to daunt or embarrass
Root: Old French esbahir (to be astonished)
That could abash the little bird
extremity (noun) · a moment of greatest hardship; the outermost point
Root: Latin extremitas, from extremus (outermost)
Yet, never, in extremity,
forbear (verb) · to hold back; to refrain
Root: Old English forberan, from for- + beran (to bear)
He that forbears To suit and serve his need,
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN

A poem about how poems are made has a Latin name. The phrase comes from the title that readers gave a verse treatise by the Roman poet Horace.

ars poetica (Latin: “the art of poetry”)
None of this week’s three poems is an ars poetica, though Blake’s questions about the hammer and the anvil treat making as a craft.
🔑 KEY TERMS
apostrophe: address to someone or something that cannot answer.
personification: giving human or living traits to an idea, object, or animal.
extended metaphor: one comparison sustained across several lines or a whole poem.
figure of speech: a use of language whose meaning goes beyond the literal sense of the words.
