Participial Phrases and Sensory Detail
Grammar + Vocab · Week 4
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A participial phrase packs an action into a description of a noun. Sensory modifiers make the description something a reader can see, hear, or feel.
Mansfield and Joyce use both. The sentences below come from this week’s stories.

📚 PART 1 · PARTICIPLES AND SENSORY MODIFIERS

A participle is a verb form that works as an adjective. The present participle ends in -ing (swooping). The past participle usually ends in -ed or -en (buckled, driven). A participial phrase is a participle plus its objects and modifiers, and the whole phrase describes a noun or pronoun.

Three rules
Place the phrase next to the noun it describes.
Set off an opening phrase with a comma: Leaning on the railing, the boy waited.
The subject that follows the comma must be the one doing the action. A mismatch leaves a dangling modifier.

Sensory modifiers are adjectives and phrases that appeal to sight, sound, smell, touch, or taste. Participles often carry them: the buckled harness adds sight and touch, playing in the sodden beds adds sound and wetness.

✎ ON THE BOARD · PARTICIPLES AND SENSORY MODIFIERS
Little children ran among them, swooping and laughing;
swooping and laughing is a present participial phrase. It modifies children and adds motion and sound.
And still soundlessly singing, still with that trembling smile, Miss Brill prepared to listen.
An opening participial phrase, set off by a comma, modifies Miss Brill. soundlessly and trembling are sensory modifiers.
leaning my forehead against the cool glass, I looked over at the dark house where she lived.
leaning my forehead against the cool glass modifies I. cool appeals to touch; dark appeals to sight.
the fine incessant needles of water playing in the sodden beds
playing in the sodden beds modifies needles. The adjectives fine, incessant, and sodden give touch, sound, and weight.
Gazing up into the darkness I saw myself as a creature driven and derided by vanity;
Gazing up into the darkness modifies I. driven and derided by vanity is a past participial phrase modifying creature.
to the dark odorous stables where a coachman smoothed and combed the horse
dark (sight) and odorous (smell) are stacked before the noun to build the place.

A comma is not always required. Joyce writes Gazing up into the darkness I saw... with no comma, because the phrase is short and the reading is clear. In formal writing the comma after an opening phrase is the safer choice.

📚 PART 2 · PRACTICE

Find the participial phrase and the noun it modifies in each sentence. Open each answer to check.

Clutching the florin, the boy hurried toward the station.
Clutching the florin modifies boy. The comma follows the opening phrase and the subject that follows is the one doing the clutching.
The band, playing louder for the Season, drew a larger crowd.
playing louder for the Season modifies band. The commas mark it as extra information, not needed to identify the band.
The fur, brushed and aired, lay in its box.
brushed and aired is a past participial phrase modifying fur.
Revise The hall was dark. Add one participial phrase and two sensory modifiers.
One possible answer: The hall, hushed after the last coins had fallen, was dark and chilly. The phrase hushed after the last coins had fallen modifies hall; dark adds sight and chilly adds touch.

A dangling modifier appears when the phrase has no sensible noun to modify: Walking through the hall, the stalls were mostly dark. The stalls cannot walk. The fix names the walker: Walking through the hall, he saw that the stalls were mostly dark.

📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from the two stories. Each entry gives the sense the story uses.

imperturbable (adjective) · not easily upset or excited; calm
Root: Latin in- (not) + perturbare (to disturb)
brown imperturbable faces (“Araby”)
garrulous (adjective) · talkative, especially about small matters
Root: Latin garrulus, from garrire (to chatter)
an old garrulous woman (“Araby”)
chafe (verb) · to feel irritated or restless under a limit
Root: Old French chaufer, from Latin calefacere (to make warm, as by rubbing)
I chafed against the work of school (“Araby”)
deride (verb) · to mock or laugh at
Root: Latin deridere, from de- + ridere (to laugh)
a creature driven and derided by vanity (“Araby”)
rotunda (noun) · a round building or structure, often domed
Root: Italian rotonda, from Latin rotundus (round)
sitting in the green rotunda (“Miss Brill”)
toque (noun) · a small, close-fitting hat without a brim
Root: French toque; the earlier source is uncertain
an ermine toque and a gentleman in grey (“Miss Brill”)
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH

English borrowed a French word for a person who strolls through a city to watch it. The word suits a reader of this week’s stories, since both Miss Brill and the boy spend much of their time observing.

flâneur (French: from flâner, “to stroll”)
A person who wanders through a city without a goal, watching the crowd. Example: The flâneur kept to the edge of the square and noted who stopped to talk and who passed by.

🔑 KEY TERMS
participle: a verb form used as an adjective, ending in -ing, -ed, or -en.
participial phrase: a participle with its objects and modifiers, describing a noun or pronoun.
dangling modifier: a modifying phrase that has no sensible noun to describe in its sentence.
sensory modifier: an adjective or phrase that appeals to sight, sound, smell, touch, or taste.
