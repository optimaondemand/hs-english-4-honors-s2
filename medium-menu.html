Five public modes
The Medium Menu
Each mode reaches a different kind of audience. Pick the one that reaches yours, then hold to its floor for the rest of the semester.
1
Vlog
Three short episodes, on camera
WHAT YOUR AUDIENCE RECEIVES
Three scripted episodes, 6 to 10 minutes each, on camera, in role.
HOW LONG OR HOW MUCH
All three episodes scripted before filming, with a published sources page.
HOW IT MEETS A PUBLIC (LESSON 7)
Posted to the class gallery discussion in Canvas, with a live or recorded delivery and real audience questions.
WHERE CLAUDE FITS
Transcript cleanup after filming. Never the script itself.
THE FILE YOU SUBMIT
Video files, or a link to the hosted episodes, plus a Word sources page.
2
Podcast
Spoken episodes, in role
WHAT YOUR AUDIENCE RECEIVES
Two episodes of 15 to 20 minutes, or one of 30, in role.
HOW LONG OR HOW MUCH
Show notes and a public sources page alongside the audio.
HOW IT MEETS A PUBLIC (LESSON 7)
Posted to the class gallery discussion in Canvas, with a live or recorded delivery and real audience questions.
WHERE CLAUDE FITS
Transcript cleanup after recording. Never the words spoken.
THE FILE YOU SUBMIT
Audio files, or a link to the hosted episodes, plus a Word show-notes and sources page.
3
Feature article
Internet news or a long-form feature
WHAT YOUR AUDIENCE RECEIVES
One long-form feature, 2,500 to 3,500 words, with a headline, a deck, pull quotes, rights-cleared images, and a sources section.
HOW LONG OR HOW MUCH
Published as a self-contained web page, built from your finished text.
HOW IT MEETS A PUBLIC (LESSON 7)
Posted to the class gallery discussion in Canvas, with a live or recorded delivery and real audience questions.
WHERE CLAUDE FITS
Page layout code, built from your finished text. Never the words of the article.
THE FILE YOU SUBMIT
The finished text in Word, plus the published web page link.
4
Imitation
A public genre, written or performed
WHAT YOUR AUDIENCE RECEIVES
A real-life public genre (testimony before a council, an op-ed series, a lecture, a campaign kit) or a genre found in your world's own texts (a dialogue, a set of sayings, an epistolary account, a council speech, a chronicle entry, an aphorism book, a feuilleton). About 3,000 words or the performed equivalent.
HOW LONG OR HOW MUCH
The genre's own conventions, followed with care, plus a public reading or performance.
HOW IT MEETS A PUBLIC (LESSON 7)
Posted to the class gallery discussion in Canvas, with a live or recorded delivery and real audience questions.
WHERE CLAUDE FITS
Fact and anachronism checks on the genre and its period. Never the words of the piece.
THE FILE YOU SUBMIT
A Word document, plus a recording of the reading or performance if the genre is performed.
5
The Address
A formal spoken case, delivered live
WHAT YOUR AUDIENCE RECEIVES
A formal public address of 15 to 20 minutes in classical structure: introduction, transitions, body, conclusion, persuasion, and rhetorical devices.
HOW LONG OR HOW MUCH
Delivered to a gathered audience, with the full text published alongside.
HOW IT MEETS A PUBLIC (LESSON 7)
Posted to the class gallery discussion in Canvas, delivered live or recorded eyes-up, with real audience questions.
WHERE CLAUDE FITS
Rehearsal: asking the objections your audience will raise. Never the words of the address.
THE FILE YOU SUBMIT
A Word document of the full text, plus a recording of the delivery.
6
The same floor for everyone
No matter which medium you choose
  • Three scholarly articles and three primary sources, used across the semester.
  • Facts checked and permissions logged twice: at Lesson 5 and again at Lesson 7.
  • The other side stated fairly, inside the work itself.
  • A look and sound grown from your world: no stock intro music, no template thumbnail.
7
One rule about formats
Your honors medium cannot repeat a format you already picked

Your honors medium may not repeat a format you pick for a quarter menu project. A podcast or speech pick blocks Podcast and The Address. A short-film pick blocks Vlog. A journal pick blocks a written Imitation.

8
Which medium reaches which audience
Three examples
ON THE BOARD
A jury of five hundred in Athens sits for hours and expects an argued case: a law-court speech reaches them, not a short clip.
A council in Geneva reads reports before it votes: a written case it can hold and reread reaches them, not a performance it has to sit for.
Readers of a Prague daily skim their paper for one sharp observation on ordinary life: a short feature reaches them, no quotation of the source text required.
