The Old Forest
Literature · Week 3
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The Old Forest is a place that was not made for travelers, and it does not pretend to be.
The hobbits expect a short cut and find a world with its own life, its own memory and its own aims. The chapter sets that world against the Shire, where nature had learned to mind its manners, and then it shows how a danger can arrive looking like rest.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe how the Forest treats the travelers and what Merry’s account says about why.
2
I can trace how the hobbits are steered from their planned route to the river.
3
I can explain how Old Man Willow’s trap works and what it shows about danger that arrives as comfort.
📋 READING
▶
Read Book I, Chapter 6, “The Old Forest”, in your copy.
📚 PART 1 · A FOREST WITH ITS OWN LIFE

The chapter begins with a crossing. Merry leads the party out of Crickhollow before dawn, through a brick tunnel under the Hedge, and an iron gate clangs shut behind them. Fatty Bolger, who is staying, says good-bye at the tunnel mouth. The narrator calls the clang of the gate ominous, and Merry says plainly that they are now outside the Shire.

Merry’s own account of the Forest is careful. He does not believe the nursery stories about goblins and wolves. He says the Forest is queer in another way: everything in it is more alive and more aware than things in the Shire, and the trees do not like strangers. They watch you. (Book I, Chapter 6) He also gives the reason. Long ago the trees massed against the Hedge, and the hobbits cut down hundreds of them and burned a long strip of the Forest. The trees gave up the attack, and they have been unfriendly since.

The hobbits soon feel the same thing from the inside. They sense that they are being watched with disapproval, deepening to dislike and even enmity (Book I, Chapter 6). Pippin shouts that he means no harm, and the cry falls as if into a curtain. The Forest gives no answer.

⚡ FIRST READ
What kind of place does the Old Forest seem to be in the hobbits’ first hours inside it?
📚 PART 2 · BEING STEERED

The route that Frodo chose is a short, simple one: through the Forest to the East Road. The Forest does not allow it. The path from the Bonfire Glade rises to a green hilltop and then bends toward the Withywindle, the valley Merry names as the source of the Forest’s queerness. When the hobbits turn north to avoid it, folds in the ground block them. Thickets give way to the right and downhill and refuse to give way to the left and uphill. The narrator reports the pattern as a fact: They were being headed off, and were simply following a course chosen for them (Book I, Chapter 6).

🗺 HOW THE FOREST STEERS THEM
1 · The gate
Merry unlocks the iron gate under the Hedge and locks it behind them.
2 · The Glade
The path to the Bonfire Glade seems to have moved, but the glade is found.
3 · The hilltop
From a bare green hill they see the fog over the Withywindle, the part they mean to avoid.
4 · The turn north
They leave a path that bends toward the river and try to go north.
5 · The folds
Hollows and thickets give way only to the right and downhill.
6 · The gully
A steep stream-bed takes them down to the valley they wished to miss.
7 · The willows
They follow a path along the river and find the great grey willow.

The hobbits have crossed a boundary from the Shire, where paths go where travelers want them to go, to a country where paths are made by something else. Pippin asks who made the track along the river and why, and Merry has no answer. The question is left to hang: someone or something has laid brushwood across the rills, and the track is not there for the visitors’ benefit.

📚 PART 3 · THE WILLOW

Old Man Willow does not attack. He sings. The afternoon is hot and the flies are loud, and then the flies fall quiet and a murmur in the branches begins to suggest water and sleep. Merry says he must have a nap. Pippin falls on his knees. Frodo notices that the others are past caring, and then he feels the same weight settle on his own eyes. Sam alone has the sense to say that something more than sun and warm air is at work.

The trap closes quietly. Frodo drifts down to a root and paddles his feet, and the root tips him into the water. Merry and Pippin lie against the trunk, and the cracks in the willow close around them with a click like a lock. Sam hauls Frodo out and then tries to burn the willow open, which makes the tree angrier. Frodo runs along the path crying for help, and a song answers from behind him.

Tom Bombadil explains the Willow’s grudge later, and it matches the Forest’s other grievance. The oldest trees remember when they were lords, and their hearts are filled with a hatred of things that go free upon the earth (Book I, Chapter 7). Tom names the Willow as the most dangerous of them, rotten at heart and cunning. He does not chase the hobbits. He lets the comfortable afternoon do the work.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Merry explains that hobbits once burned a strip of the Forest. What does the account show about the trees’ attitude?
2. The willow’s trap begins with sleepiness and singing. What does the order of events suggest?
3. Frodo chose the Forest to avoid the Riders. What does the day show about the plan?
🔑 KEY TERMS
hostile: unfriendly, or acting as an enemy.
grievance: a wrong that someone has not forgotten.
Hedge: the tall boundary hedge between Buckland and the Forest.
hospitality: the welcome given to guests; the Forest has none to give.
