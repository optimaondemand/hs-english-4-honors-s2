📚 PART 1 · WHAT A RECITATION IS

A recitation is a passage learned by heart and delivered aloud without notes. The memory has to hold the words, their order, and the places where the voice pauses, rises and drops. Recitation also shows how a passage is built, because a speaker who knows it by heart has to know why each part follows the one before.

Two kinds of passage appear on the menu. Hughes and Herbert are verse: the line length and the pattern of the stanza do part of the remembering. Plato is a spoken exchange in prose: two voices trade turns, and the memory has to carry the order of the questions and answers.

📚 PART 2 · THE PASSAGE MENU (CHOOSE ONE)

Read every passage aloud once before choosing. Choose the one that is easiest to say with conviction.

📜 PASSAGE MENU
OPTION A · Hughes, “Epilogue” (“I, Too”)
18 lines, whole poem. The poem is titled “Epilogue” in its first edition (1926) and is now known as “I, Too.”
I, too, sing America.


I am the darker brother.
They send me to eat in the kitchen
When company comes,
But I laugh,
And eat well,
And grow strong.


Tomorrow,
I’ll sit at the table
When company comes.
Nobody’ll dare
Say to me,
“Eat in the kitchen,”
Then.


Besides,
They’ll see how beautiful I am
And be ashamed,—


I, too, am America.
Langston Hughes, “Epilogue,” The Weary Blues (1926; public domain)
OPTION B · Herbert, “The Collar”
36 lines, whole poem, in its original seventeenth-century spelling. The speaker’s “My Lord” is the Christian God.
I struck the board, and cry'd, No more;
I will abroad.
What? shall I ever sigh and pine?
My lines and life are free; free as the rode,
Loose as the winde, as large as store.
Shall I be still in suit?
Have I no harvest but a thorn
To let me bloud, and not restore
What I have lost with cordiall fruit?
Sure there was wine
Before my sighs did drie it: there was corn
Before my tears did drown it.
Is the yeare onely lost to me?
Have I no bayes to crown it?
No flowers, no garlands gay? all blasted?
All wasted?
Not so, my heart: but there is fruit,
And thou hast hands.
Recover all thy sigh-blown age
On double pleasures: leave thy cold dispute
Of what is fit, and not. Forsake thy cage,
Thy rope of sands,
Which pettie thoughts have made, and made to thee
Good cable, to enforce and draw,
And be thy law,
While thou didst wink and wouldst not see.
Away; take heed,
I will abroad.
Call in thy deaths head there: tie up thy fears.
He that forbears
To suit and serve his need,
Deserves his load.
But as I rav'd and grew more fierce and wilde
At every word,
Me thoughts I heard one calling, Childe:
And I reply'd, My Lord.
George Herbert, “The Collar,” The Temple (1633; public domain)
OPTION C · Plato, the return to the cave
140 words of prose. Socrates and Glaucon on what happens when the freed prisoner goes back down into the den.
Imagine once more, I said, such a one coming suddenly out of the sun to be replaced in his old situation; would he not be certain to have his eyes full of darkness?

To be sure, he said.

And if there were a contest, and he had to compete in measuring the shadows with the prisoners who had never moved out of the den, while his sight was still weak, and before his eyes had become steady, would he not be ridiculous? Men would say of him that up he went and down he came without his eyes, and that it was better not even to think of ascending; and if anyone tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death.

No question, he said.
Plato, Republic, Book VII, the Allegory of the Cave (Jowett translation; public domain)
📚 PART 3 · HOW TO PRACTICE

Weeks of ungraded practice come before the recording. The routine below fits both verse and prose.

1
Memorize in chunks. Break verse where the lines break and the Plato passage where each speaker’s turn ends. Master two or three, then link them to the next.
2
Say it aloud every time. Silent reading builds a different memory from speaking.
3
Let punctuation mark the breath. A comma is a short pause and a full stop is a longer one. In verse, a line break is a place to breathe even when the sentence goes on.
4
Mark a copy. Put // at each full stop, / at each short pause, and an underline under the word that carries each sentence. For the Plato passage, mark each change of speaker.
5
Test from memory. Say the passage with the page turned over, and mark where the memory slipped. Return to those places first.
6
Record a practice take. Listen once for pace and volume before the Week 8 recording.
