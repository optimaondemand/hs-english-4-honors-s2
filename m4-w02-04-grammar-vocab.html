Grammar + Vocab · Week 2
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A participial phrase is a verb form, built on an -ing or -ed word, that does the work of an adjective.
The phrase describes a noun and has to sit next to it. Tolkien’s chapters on the road use these phrases to put action and description in one sentence, and a writer analyzing the chapters can borrow the habit.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify a participial phrase and the noun it describes.
2
I can place a participial phrase next to the noun it modifies and punctuate it.
3
I can repair a dangling participial phrase.
4
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · THE SKILL: PARTICIPIAL PHRASES

A participle is a verb form used as an adjective. A present participle ends in -ing (hiding, sniffing); a past participle usually ends in -ed or -en (wrapped, frozen) or has an irregular form. A participial phrase is the participle with its objects and modifiers: hiding behind a tree root. The phrase describes a noun, and the noun is who or what is hiding.

When the phrase opens a sentence, a comma follows it, and the noun it describes should come right after the comma. When the phrase interrupts a sentence and is not needed to say which noun is meant, commas set it off on both sides. A phrase that stands next to the wrong noun, or has no noun to describe, is a dangling or misplaced modifier.

✎ ON THE BOARD · PARTICIPIAL PHRASES AT WORK
Hiding behind a tree root, Frodo watched the rider pass.
Hiding behind a tree root describes Frodo. Opening phrase, comma, then the noun it describes.
The rider, wrapped in a black cloak, sat quite still.
Wrapped in a black cloak is a past participial phrase describing rider. Commas set it off on both sides.
Pippin, dazed with sleep, staggered along the lane.
Dazed with sleep describes Pippin.
Touched with gold and red the autumn trees seemed to be sailing rootless in a shadowy sea. (Book I, Chapter 3)
From Chapter 3. Touched with gold and red is a past participial phrase describing the autumn trees.
Frodo came out drying his hair. (Book I, Chapter 5)
From Chapter 5. Drying his hair is a present participial phrase describing Frodo.
Sitting in the hollow tree, the road was out of sight.
Dangling. The road cannot sit in a tree. Repair: Sitting in the hollow tree, the hobbits could not see the road.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from Chapters 3 to 5. Each entry gives the sense Tolkien’s narrator uses.

reluctant (adjective) · unwilling; holding back
Root: Latin reluctari, to struggle against, from re- (against) + luctari (to wrestle).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 3
elusive (adjective) · hard to find, catch or keep hold of
Root: Latin eludere, to escape by a trick, from e- (out) + ludere (to play).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 3
frugal (adjective) · sparing; using little
Root: Latin frugalis, from frux (fruit, produce): a person who lives on simple produce.
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 3
inquisitive (adjective) · eager to find things out, often too eager
Root: Latin inquirere, to seek into, from in- + quaerere (to seek).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapters 3 and 5
disconcerting (adjective) · upsetting a person’s calm or plans
Root: French déconcerter, from dis- (undo) + concerter (to bring into harmony).
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 4
scrupulous (adjective) · careful about what is right; exact
Root: Latin scrupulus, a small sharp stone, used for a nagging doubt about right and wrong.
Where it appears: Book I, Chapter 5
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH

English borrowed this French phrase for complete freedom to act as one sees fit, as if one had been handed a blank sheet to fill in.

carte blanche (French: “white card”)
Gandalf gave Frodo carte blanche on how to leave the Shire, but not on how long to wait.
🔑 KEY TERMS
participle: a verb form that acts as an adjective.
participial phrase: a participle with its objects and modifiers, describing a noun.
dangling modifier: a modifier with no noun nearby that it can sensibly describe.
modifier: a word or phrase that describes another word.
