📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Part II is full of ordinary things that carry more than their weight: the picture, the furniture, the apple, the sheet, the door.
A symbol is a concrete thing that stands for an idea. A motif is an element that recurs. The lesson shows how to read each from the scene it appears in, and Word Study takes six words from Part II.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can distinguish a symbol from a motif.
2
I can read a symbol’s meaning from its context in a scene.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · SYMBOL AND MOTIF
Symbol and motif
Symbol: a concrete thing (an object, an action, an image) that also stands for an abstract idea. It can appear once.
Motif: an element that recurs across a work and builds meaning by repetition.
Reading rule: ask what the thing does in the scene, then what idea that action points to.

Two habits keep a reading honest. The meaning must grow out of what happens in the scene, not from what a reader wishes it meant. And more than one shade of meaning is allowed, but not any meaning: the strongest readings are the ones the details support best.

✎ ON THE BOARD · FOUR READINGS FROM PART II
The apple comes from the family’s own bowl, and in the father’s hand it wounds: ordinary nourishment becomes a sign of rejection.
Symbol, read from context: a household’s care turned into harm.
Gregor covers the framed picture with his body while the room is emptied around him.
Symbol, read from context: the picture stands for the human self he refuses to surrender.
Doors are opened, shut, locked, and unlocked around Gregor, and each change decides who may reach him.
Motif: doors recur through the part and track the family’s distance.
Grete throws the window open the moment she enters, and Gregor, propped on a chair at the sill, stares at a street he can barely make out.
Motif: windows and sight recur, one for her relief and one for his shrinking world.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from Part II. Translations differ, so a word may not appear in a given edition, but each entry names the part of the story it belongs to.

solicitude (noun) · anxious care or concern for someone
Root: Latin sollicitudo, from sollicitus (agitated), from sollus (whole) + citus (set in motion)
Where: Part II, Grete’s early handling of Gregor’s food and room
revulsion (noun) · a sudden, strong feeling of disgust
Root: Latin revellere (to pull away), from re- + vellere (to pluck)
Where: Part II, Grete at the window and her unease at the sight of him
dismantle (verb) · to take apart or strip of its contents
Root: Old French desmanteler (to strip of a cloak), from des- + manteler
Where: Part II, the women emptying the room
tentative (adjective) · hesitant; done to test or try something
Root: Latin tentare (to try, to test)
Where: Part II, the first approaches of Grete and the mother to Gregor’s room
oppress (verb) · to weigh heavily on; to burden with anxiety or hardship
Root: Latin opprimere (to press down), from ob- + premere (to press)
Where: Part II, Gregor’s anxiety as the room is emptied
indispensable (adjective) · too important to be done without
Root: Latin in- (not) + dispensare (to weigh out, to distribute)
Where: Part II, Gregor’s judgment that the desk and the picture must stay
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM GERMAN

German gave English several words for the uncanny and the divided self. This one names a double of a living person, an exact copy who appears as a stranger.

doppelgänger (German: “double-goer”)
Sam stopped short in the hallway: the figure in the mirror copied every step, a doppelgänger who seemed to belong to the house more than he did.
🔑 KEY TERMS
symbol: a concrete thing that also stands for an abstract idea.
motif: an element that recurs across a work and builds meaning by repetition.
context: the scene and action around a detail, which fix what it can mean.
