📚 PART 1 · THE BOY IN THE PARLOR
“A boy is in the parlor what the pit is in the playhouse; independent, irresponsible, looking out from his corner on such people and facts as pass by, he tries and sentences them on their merits, in the swift, summary way of boys, as good, bad, interesting, silly, eloquent, troublesome. He cumbers himself never about consequences about interests; he gives an independent, genuine verdict. You must court him: he does not court you. But the man is, as it were, clapped into jail by his consciousness. As soon as he has once acted or spoken with éclat he is a committed person, watched by the sympathy or the hatred of hundreds, whose affections must now enter into his account. There is no Lethe for this. Ah, that he could pass again into his neutrality!”
Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841; public domain)

Emerson’s model of independence is a boy. He sits in the parlor as a spectator sits in the pit of a theater, and he gives “an independent, genuine verdict.” The man differs because he has acted in public. Once he has spoken with éclat (brilliance, public success), he is “a committed person, watched by the sympathy or the hatred of hundreds.” Being watched is the mechanism. Attention from others brings obligations, and a reputation then governs what the man can say.

Miss Brill takes the opposite view of being watched. The idea that she is part of a performance gives her a place among the people in the garden. The story shows what that dependence on an audience costs when the audience speaks.

📚 PART 2 · THE JOINT-STOCK COMPANY
“These are the voices which we hear in solitude, but they grow faint and inaudible as we enter into the world. Society everywhere is in conspiracy against the manhood of every one of its members. Society is a joint-stock company, in which the members agree, for the better securing of his bread to each shareholder, to surrender the liberty and culture of the eater. The virtue in most request is conformity. Self-reliance is its aversion. It loves not realities and creators, but names and customs.”
Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841; public domain)

Emerson’s charge is deliberately strong: “Society everywhere is in conspiracy against the manhood of every one of its members.” The image that follows makes the arrangement plain. A joint-stock company is a business whose members pool their efforts for a shared return. Here the return is security, “the better securing of his bread to each shareholder”, and the price is “the liberty and culture of the eater.” The member who eats gives up the freedom to think and grow in exchange for food and standing.

The virtue society rewards is conformity, and “Self-reliance is its aversion.” Society “loves not realities and creators, but names and customs.” The sentence uses a contrast: realities and creators on one side, names and customs on the other. A custom is a repeated practice, and a name is a label that stands in for the thing named. Gregor’s life in Part I is a small version of the bargain: his wages and his place at the firm secure the household, and the arrangement leaves almost nothing of him outside the job.

📚 PART 3 · THE INTEGRITY OF YOUR OWN MIND
“Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist. He who would gather immortal palms must not be hindered by the name of goodness, but must explore if it be goodness. Nothing is at last sacred but the integrity of your own mind. Absolve you to yourself, and you shall have the suffrage of the world.”
Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841; public domain)

“Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist.” The essay uses man for the full human person. The next sentence shows what nonconformity means in practice. A person who wants lasting honor (palms were a classical emblem of victory) “must not be hindered by the name of goodness, but must explore if it be goodness.” A name such as goodness is a label supplied by custom, and the person is to examine the thing itself. The demand is not to oppose every custom. It is to test each one.

“Nothing is at last sacred but the integrity of your own mind” sets the test in the person’s own honesty. The promise follows: “Absolve you to yourself, and you shall have the suffrage of the world.” Suffrage here means approval, so a person at peace with his own judgment will win support in the end.

The claim has an edge. If nothing is sacred but one’s own mind, no outside standard decides when that mind is wrong. The excerpt does not say who judges.

⚖ TWO WAYS OF LIVING, AS EMERSON FRAMES THEM

What it loves“names and customs”“realities and creators”
What it offersSecurity and standing for members who agree.Honesty and wholeness for a person who tests each claim.
What it costsThe member’s own perception, traded for approval.The risk of standing apart and of being wrong with no outside check.

🔑 KEY TERMS
conformity: behavior that matches the group’s expectations.
nonconformist: a person who tests the group’s customs instead of accepting them by habit.
integrity: wholeness; firm adherence to one’s own honest judgment.
