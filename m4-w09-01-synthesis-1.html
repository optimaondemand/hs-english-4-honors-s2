📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The year’s texts answer one question from four directions: what is owed, and to whom.
The module asks what is owed when a problem belongs to everyone. The first three quarters ask it indirectly, about makers and what they make, about monsters and humans, and about the self alone and the self among others. This lesson draws those answers together.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can state how the year’s texts treat the good as given and as made.
2
I can explain where each text places the fault when something is called a monster.
3
I can compare Gregor alone with Frodo accompanied, and state what the comparison shows about what is owed.

📚 PART 1 · GIVEN AND MADE

The first quarter read seven traditions: the Bhagavad Gita, the Bible, the Confucian Analects, the Buddhist parables, the Qur’an, Plato, and Aristotle. They differ widely, and each treats the good as something that comes before the person. It is laid out by a teacher or a scripture, learned as a way, or built into what a human being is. Aristotle adds that virtue is made by practice, though toward an end that the human nature supplies.

Plato’s cave adds a duty to the others. The freed prisoner has seen more than his old companions, and the text says what it costs to go back:

“...if anyone tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death.”
Plato, Republic VII, trans. Jowett (public domain)

The second quarter turned to the good as something made. Frankenstein shows a maker who walks away from what he made, and a made creature who asks who else is to blame. Nietzsche asks who decides what is good and answers that values have a history and can be made anew, with a warning that whoever fights monsters risks becoming one. Blake’s question from the third quarter belongs with these:

Did He who made the lamb make thee?
Blake, “The Tyger”, Songs of Experience (public domain)

Read together, the two sets say that a given good can be held without being understood and that a made good can be made by the strong for themselves alone. In both, the maker or the teacher owes something to whoever stands under them, and the one who stands under them owes something back.

📚 PART 2 · MONSTER AND HUMAN

Each quarter puts a figure in front of the reader whom others call a monster. The texts do not agree on where the fault lies, and they share one habit: the monstrous is located in conduct more often than in bodies.

The creature is made, then shunned, and the novel lets him say so in the last pages. Gregor takes an insect’s body, and the story keeps its attention on the household that valued him as a source of income and then withdrew care as he failed. Gollum is twisted by the Ring over a long age, and Bilbo pities him and spares his life. Boromir is not monstrous in body or origin. He does a monstrous thing from a real loyalty with nothing above it, and his horror afterward is real. Galadriel shows the other side: she is offered the Ring freely, names her desire, and sets it down.

The comparison does not excuse anyone. The creature kills, Gollum murders, and Boromir lays hands on a friend. It shows that in each case the harm grows from a relation that failed, and that the failure had more than one party.

📚 PART 3 · THE CALL AND THE SELF AMONG OTHERS

The third quarter set two answers to the question of how a self should stand. Emerson makes the individual’s own mind the court of last appeal:

“Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist.”
Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841; public domain)

Marcus Aurelius places the self inside a whole:

“...for we are all born to be fellow-workers, as the feet, the hands, and the eyelids...”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (public domain)

Gregor stands between the two without being able to choose. His isolation is imposed by others, and the story leaves open whether his end is a gift of self or an erasure of self. Miss Brill and the boy in “Araby” also stand alone in company. Herbert’s poem gives the turn that these figures never get, a revolt that ends with an answer to a call:

Me thoughts I heard one calling, Childe:
And I reply'd, My Lord.
George Herbert, “The Collar” (1633; public domain)

The fourth quarter gives the call a communal form. At the Council the Ring is everyone’s problem and no one people’s to carry, and no one can lay it on another. The Company is neither chosen nor inherited. Each member comes by an individual call that joins a personal purpose, loyalty to a people, and a higher claim of what is right, and no oath binds them. Frodo takes the task, Sam goes where Frodo goes, and Aragorn declines to force the choice. When the Company breaks, the call does not.

📚 PART 4 · WHAT WE OWE

The year’s answers can be set in one table. Each row names the texts that speak to a question and the claim they support.

⚖ THE YEAR IN FOUR ANSWERS
QuarterTextsWhat is owedTo whom
1 · Ancient WisdomThe seven traditions; Plato’s caveA life shaped by a good that precedes the self, and a return to those still in the darkTeachers, neighbours, and those who have not yet seen
2 · Creature and CreatorFrankenstein; NietzscheCare for what one has made, and an honest account of who decides what is goodThe made, and those who live under the maker’s values
3 · What We BecomeThe Metamorphosis; Emerson; Marcus; the poemsRecognition of the person, and a self that answers a call beyond itselfThe one in the room, and the whole of which one is a part
4 · The FellowshipThe Fellowship of the RingAn answer to a call that belongs to no one people and cannot be forcedEveryone, through the person who is nearest and the task that is one’s own

Gregor alone and Frodo accompanied are the clearest pair in the table. Gregor is shut away and the people who owed him recognition withdraw it. Frodo goes on alone for everyone’s sake, and Sam refuses to let him. The difference is not in the burden, which each carries for others. It lies in whether someone comes to the door. The Company shows that a duty can be answered freely and still be shared, and that what is owed to a person includes company on the way.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the texts best support.
1. Nietzsche holds that values can be made, and Frankenstein shows a maker who disowns what he made. Which claim do the two together best support?
2. The creature, Gregor, and Gollum are each treated as a monster. What do the texts share in placing the fault?
3. The Fellowship was neither chosen nor inherited. Which statement fits the way its members came together?
🔑 TAKEAWAY
A given good and a made good both carry duties: the maker and the teacher owe care, and those who stand under them owe an answer.
The texts place the monstrous in a failed relation more often than in a body, and none excuses the harm.
A call that belongs to everyone cannot be forced on anyone, and it is carried best with company.
