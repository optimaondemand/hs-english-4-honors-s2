The Synthesis Thesis and Transitions
Grammar + Vocab · Week 8
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A synthesis claims how texts relate, and transitions show the relation.
A strong synthesis does not set two texts side by side. It states a claim about how they relate, and each transition tells the reader whether the next sentence adds, contrasts, explains, or concedes. The sentences on the board are original and about the module’s texts.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can tell a list from a thesis that claims a relationship.
2
I can name and use transitions of addition, contrast, cause, and concession.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · THESIS AND TRANSITIONS

A list says what each text is about. A synthesis thesis says how the texts relate, and a reasonable reader could dispute it. The test: if no one could disagree, it is a summary.

Transitions are the joints of the argument. Addition or similarity (likewise, in the same way) extends a point. Contrast (by contrast, where one text… the other…) sets views against each other. Cause (as a result, which is why) gives what follows. Concession (although, even so) grants a point and then limits it. A concession is a strong move in synthesis, since it shows the writer has weighed the evidence against the claim.

✎ ON THE BOARD · A LIST, A THESIS, AND FOUR TRANSITIONS
Emerson writes about the self, and Marcus writes about duty.
List. It names what each text is about and makes no claim about how they relate. No reasonable reader could dispute it.
Emerson places a person’s authority in the self, while Marcus places it in the whole the self belongs to, and Gregor’s story shows what each leaves unanswered when no one recognizes the self.
Synthesis thesis. It claims a relationship and can be disputed. The rest of the writing has to defend it.
Likewise, Herbert’s speaker ends by answering a call he did not choose.
Addition or similarity. The sentence extends the point just made.
By contrast, Emerson treats the crowd as the danger to the self.
Contrast. The sentence sets two views against each other.
As a result, the household’s care for Gregor shrinks to what it can afford.
Cause and effect. The sentence names what follows from what came before.
Although Marcus counts separation as a fault, Gregor’s separation is imposed, so the fault lies elsewhere.
Concession. The sentence grants a point, then limits it.

📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from the module’s readings in Emerson, Marcus, Blake, and Herbert. Each entry gives the sense the text uses.

conformity (noun) · agreement with the customs or opinions of a group
Root: Latin conformitas, from conformare (to shape alike), from con- + formare
The virtue in most request is conformity.
integrity (noun) · wholeness; soundness of principle
Root: Latin integritas, from integer (whole, untouched), from in- + tangere (to touch)
Nothing is at last sacred but the integrity of your own mind.
magnanimous (adjective) · generous in spirit; large-souled
Root: Latin magnanimus, from magnus (great) + animus (soul)
hinder thee from being just? or magnanimous?
contemptible (adjective) · deserving scorn
Root: Latin contemptibilis, from contemnere (to despise)
they are in themselves but vain, putrid, contemptible.
immortal (adjective) · not subject to death; lasting forever
Root: Latin immortalis, from in- (not) + mortalis (mortal)
What immortal hand or eye
dispute (noun) · an argument or disagreement
Root: Latin disputare (to weigh, discuss), from dis- + putare (to reckon)
leave thy cold dispute
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH

A writer who asks what a text or a life is for is asking for its reason for being. English kept the French phrase.

raison d’être (French: “reason for being”)
For Marcus the raison d’être of a person is work for the common good, and for Emerson it is the integrity of the individual mind.
🔑 KEY TERMS
thesis: a claim the writing sets out to defend.
synthesis: writing that brings several texts into one claim about how they relate.
transition: a word or phrase that shows how one sentence relates to the last.
concession: a point granted before the writer limits it.
