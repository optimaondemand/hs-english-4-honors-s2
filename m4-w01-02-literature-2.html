The Shire and the Cave
Literature · Week 1
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Plato’s cave pictures people who take shadows for the real world. The Shire can be read against it, with care at the points where the picture does not fit.
The image returns from earlier in the year. Here it is a tool: each comparison has to be backed by evidence from the Prologue and the first two chapters, and the places where the comparison breaks are as useful as the places where it holds.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe the cave using Plato’s own words.
2
I can apply the image to the Shire with evidence from the Prologue and Chapters 1 and 2.
3
I can identify where the comparison fails and say what the failure shows.

📜 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Plato was an Athenian philosopher of the fourth century BCE. The Republic is a dialogue in which Socrates, Plato’s teacher, talks with Glaucon, Plato’s brother. Book VII begins with the cave, which Socrates offers as a picture of how far people are enlightened or unenlightened. The translation below is by Benjamin Jowett (1871).
📚 PART 1 · THE CAVE

Socrates asks Glaucon to imagine people who have lived since childhood in an underground den, chained so that they can face only the wall in front of them. Behind them a fire burns, and between the fire and the prisoners people carry objects along a low wall, so that the shadows of the objects fall on the wall the prisoners face.

Behold! human beings living in an underground den, which has a mouth open towards the light and reaching all along the den;
Plato, Republic, Book VII, the Allegory of the Cave, trans. Jowett (public domain)

The prisoners can name only what they see, and what they see is shadow. Socrates draws the conclusion in one sentence:

To them, I said, the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images.
Plato, Republic, Book VII, the Allegory of the Cave, trans. Jowett (public domain)

The point of the picture is that a whole way of life can rest on a limited view without anyone feeling the limit. The prisoners are not unhappy. They have honors and skills, and these are measured by how well a person reads the shadows.

📚 PART 2 · THE ASCENT AND THE RETURN

Socrates then imagines one prisoner freed and forced to turn toward the light. The change is painful, and the freed man is more likely to doubt the new sight than the old:

he will suffer sharp pains; the glare will distress him, and he will be unable to see the realities of which in his former state he had seen the shadows;
Plato, Republic, Book VII, the Allegory of the Cave, trans. Jowett (public domain)

Only slowly does he learn to see the sun itself. When he goes back down, his eyes are full of darkness, and the others do not welcome what he brings:

Men would say of him that up he went and down he came without his eyes, and that it was better not even to think of ascending;
Plato, Republic, Book VII, the Allegory of the Cave, trans. Jowett (public domain)

Socrates ends by explaining the figure: the prison is the world of sight, the fire is the sun, and the climb is the soul’s ascent into the world of thought. Three features matter for the Shire: the comfort of the prisoners, the pain of the first sight of the truth, and the ridicule that waits for the one who brings news.

📚 PART 3 · WHERE THE COMPARISON HOLDS AND WHERE IT BREAKS

The strongest evidence for the comparison is how the hobbits talk about the world. The Prologue ends its account of the Shire with a judgment on the people:

until they came to think that peace and plenty were the rule in Middle-earth and the right of all sensible folk (Prologue)

In Chapter 1 the Gaffer gives the same belief a practical form when he recalls what he told his son to stay away from:

Elves and Dragons! I says to him. Cabbages and potatoes are better for me and you. (Book I, Chapter 1)

In Chapter 2 the scene at The Green Dragon shows the ridicule. Sam reports a giant striding across the northern moors and Elves sailing west, and the miller’s son laughs it off. He dismisses Bilbo and Frodo as both cracked (Book I, Chapter 2). The prisoners’ verdict on the one who comes back from the light is already in place before anyone has gone anywhere.

Then Gandalf plays the guide. He shows Frodo the Ring’s fire-letters, tells the history of the Enemy, and leaves nothing comfortable standing. Frodo’s first reactions follow Plato’s freed prisoner: he is frightened, he wishes the whole matter had never reached him, and he tries to throw the Ring into the fire and finds that he cannot.

⚖ THE CAVE AND THE SHIRE, SIDE BY SIDE
In PlatoIn the ShireHow well it fits
Chains that hold the prisoners facing the wallHabit, contentment, and a settled dislike of what lies outside the bordersWeak. Nothing forces a hobbit to stay.
Shadows taken for the real worldThe belief that peace and plenty are the rule everywhereStrong. The Prologue states it.
The freed prisoner, pained by the lightFrodo, shaken by what Gandalf shows him of the Ring and the EnemyStrong. His fear and his wish to hand the burden back follow the pattern.
The guide who drags him up the slopeGandalf, who tells the whole history and then says the decision lies with FrodoPartial. Gandalf will not force him.
The prisoner who goes back down and is mockedBilbo and Frodo, called cracked in HobbitonPartial. The mockery is present; the return is not yet.

The comparison breaks at two points. First, the Shire’s peace is not an illusion. It is real, and it rests on work that the hobbits do not notice. The Prologue says they forgot the Guardians who made the long peace possible, and Gandalf later tells Frodo that watchful eyes have guarded the Shire every day he was away. The Shire is less a cave of false belief than a house whose foundations are out of sight.

Second, Plato’s freed prisoner pities those left below. Frodo’s answer to the same knowledge is different. He says he wants to save the Shire, and he concludes that he must leave it so that the danger follows him instead of staying among the people he loves. His new sight does not lift him above the Shire. It binds him to it.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Plato’s freed prisoner wants to turn back from the light. Which moment in Chapter 2 is the closest parallel?
2. Where does the cave comparison fail most clearly?
3. Frodo plans to leave the Shire to draw danger away from it. How does this differ from Plato’s freed prisoner?
🔑 KEY TERMS
allegory: a story or picture in which each part stands for an idea.
illusion: something that appears real and is not.
ascent: a climb upward; in Plato, the mind’s movement from appearance to understanding.
Guardians: in the Prologue, those who kept the Shire safe and are forgotten by the hobbits.
