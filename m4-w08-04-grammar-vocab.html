Parallel Structure and Antithesis
Matching form for matching and opposed ideas, with Word Study from Chapters 8 to 10.
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Ideas of equal weight take the same grammatical form, and opposed ideas can take it too.
Parallel structure sets matching ideas in matching grammar. Antithesis places opposed ideas in matching grammar so that the contrast shows. Both depend on the reader being able to see the pattern at once.

📚 PART 1 · MATCHING FORM

Parallel structure uses the same pattern for each item: three verbs in the same form, two phrases that start the same way, or clauses that repeat a frame. A reader who sees the first item expects the others to follow it, and a break in the pattern reads as a mistake. Antithesis uses the same pattern for two ideas that oppose each other, so the form shows the meaning.

✎ ON THE BOARD · PATTERNS IN FORM
The fearless, the ruthless, these alone will achieve victory.
Two matching noun phrases build to a summing-up. The pattern makes a doubtful claim sound settled.
Frodo carries the Ring; Sam carries the pack; Aragorn carries the grief.
Parallel. The repeated verb gives each burden equal weight, and only the object changes.
Boromir wanted a weapon to win the war; Frodo wanted an end to the war.
Antithesis. Matching clauses set opposite aims against each other.
Faulty: Sam guessed the plan, ran to the boats, and his leap into the river nearly drowned him.
Revised: Sam guessed the plan, ran to the boats, and nearly drowned in the river.
The faulty version changes form in the third item. The revision keeps three verbs in a row.
Torment in the dark was the danger that I feared, and it did not hold me back. But I would not have come, had I known the danger of light and joy.
Antithesis. Dark and torment are set against light and joy in the same frame, and the second half overturns the expectation set by the first.

To test a sentence, line up the items and compare their forms. To read antithesis, name the two opposed terms and the pattern that joins them, and then ask what the pattern makes the reader feel.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from Chapters 8, 9, and 10. Each entry gives the sense the book uses, and the chapter where it appears.

heirloom (noun) · a valued object passed down in a family
Root: heir + Old English geloma, a tool or implement
Appears in: Chapter 8
portage (noun) · the carrying of boats and goods overland between waters
Root: Latin portare, to carry, through French
Appears in: Chapter 9
desolate (adjective) · empty, bare, and without life
Root: Latin desolare, from de- + solus, alone
Appears in: Chapter 9
forebode (verb) · to be a sign of trouble to come
Root: fore- + Old English bodian, to announce
Appears in: Chapter 9
benevolent (adjective) · wishing and doing good to others
Root: Latin benevolens, from bene, well, + volens, wishing
Appears in: Chapter 10
ruthless (adjective) · without pity
Root: ruth, pity, from Middle English ruen, to rue, + -less
Appears in: Chapter 10
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH

English borrowed this phrase for the loyalty that holds a group together under strain.

esprit de corps (French: “spirit of the body”; the shared morale and loyalty of a group)
Merry and Pippin’s refusal to leave Frodo shows an esprit de corps that no order from Aragorn created.
🔑 KEY TERMS
parallel structure: matching grammatical form for matching ideas.
antithesis: opposed ideas set in matching form so that the contrast shows.
faulty parallelism: a change of grammatical form partway through a series.
