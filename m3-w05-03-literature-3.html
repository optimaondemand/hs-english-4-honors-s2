📚 PART 1 · THE FRIEND’S OBJECTION
“I remember an answer which when quite young I was prompted to make to a valued adviser, who was wont to importune me with the dear old doctrines of the church. On my saying, What have I to do with the sacredness of traditions, if I live wholly from within? my friend suggested: ‘But these impulses may be from below, not from above.’ I replied: ‘They do not seem to me to be such; but if I am the Devil’s child, I will live then from the Devil.’ No law can be sacred to me but that of my nature. Good and bad are but names very readily transferable to that or this; the only right is what is after my constitution, the only wrong what is against it.”
Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841; public domain)

The adviser speaks from the doctrine of the church of Emerson’s day. His objection is that the impulses might come “from below, not from above”, that is, from a lower or evil source and not from God. Emerson’s reply is not an argument. He says the impulses “do not seem to me to be such” and adds that if he is “the Devil’s child”, he will “live then from the Devil.” The next sentence states the rule: “No law can be sacred to me but that of my nature.” Right is “what is after my constitution”, meaning his own make-up, and wrong is what runs against it.

The reply is a wager on his own honesty. It gives no way to tell, from inside, an impulse from above from one from below.

⚡ FIRST READ
Which statement describes Emerson’s reply to the friend’s objection?
📚 PART 2 · A FOOLISH CONSISTENCY
“A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines. With consistency a great soul has simply nothing to do. He may as well concern himself with the shadow on the wall. Speak what you think now in hard words, and to-morrow speak what to-morrow thinks in hard words again, though it contradict everything you said to-day.—‘Ah, so you shall be sure to be misunderstood.’—Is it so bad, then, to be misunderstood? Pythagoras was misunderstood, and Socrates, and Jesus, and Luther, and Copernicus, and Galileo, and Newton, and every pure and wise spirit that ever took flesh. To be great is to be misunderstood.”
Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841; public domain)

A hobgoblin is a bugbear, something that causes needless fear. Emerson says the fear of contradicting oneself keeps people tied to yesterday’s opinions. He proposes to “Speak what you think now in hard words” and let tomorrow’s thought contradict it. The point is a fair one: growth requires giving up old positions, and honesty sometimes costs the look of consistency.

The line “To be great is to be misunderstood” is supported by a list: Pythagoras, Socrates, Jesus, Luther, Copernicus, Galileo, Newton. The list shows that great people have been misunderstood. It does not show that everyone who is misunderstood is great. The reading in which unpopularity proves worth reverses the sentence and makes the claim false.

📚 PART 3 · THE SELF THAT ANSWERS ONLY TO ITSELF
“I shun father and mother and wife and brother, when my genius calls me. I would write on the lintels of the door-post, Whim. I hope it is somewhat better than whim at last, but we cannot spend the day in explanation. Expect me not to show cause why I seek or why I exclude company.”
Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841; public domain)

Elsewhere in the essay Emerson applies the principle to family and company. He claims the right to “shun father and mother and wife and brother” when his genius calls him, and to withhold his reasons: “Expect me not to show cause why I seek or why I exclude company.” Taken at full strength, the principle makes a person’s own nature the only court.

The strength is real. A self that checks its own perception honestly resists flattery and fashion. The risk is that nothing outside the self checks the picture. Two characters show both sides.

🔑 KEY TERMS
hobgoblin: a bugbear; something that causes needless fear.
consistency: agreement between what a person says now and what he said before.
constitution: a person’s basic make-up or nature.
