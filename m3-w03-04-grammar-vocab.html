📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Part III depends on irony: a loving act follows a verdict that Gregor is not Gregor, and an ending in the language of spring follows a death.
Irony has three main kinds, each defined by a gap. The lesson names them and shows each at work in the story. Word Study takes six words from Part III.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can distinguish verbal, situational, and dramatic irony.
2
I can identify an example of each kind in the story.
3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · THREE KINDS OF IRONY
Three kinds of irony
Verbal: a speaker says one thing and means the opposite.
Situational: what happens is the opposite of what was expected or fitting.
Dramatic: the reader knows something a character does not.
✎ ON THE BOARD · THREE GAPS
A reader might remark that the firm showed great concern for Gregor, meaning that it showed none.
Verbal. The words say one thing and the meaning is the opposite.
A family decides Gregor is not Gregor, and within hours it receives the one act it had demanded of the real one.
Situational. The outcome reverses what the family meant.
The reader hears Gregor’s love for his family while the family hears only noise.
Dramatic. The reader knows what the characters do not.
Warm sunshine and plans for Grete’s future follow a death in the flat.
Situational. The tone of renewal sits beside a loss, and the narration does not comment.

A common error is to call any coincidence or piece of bad luck ironic. Irony requires a meaningful gap: between what is said and what is meant, between what is expected and what happens, or between what the reader knows and what a character knows.

⚡ TRY IT · WHICH KIND OF IRONY?
Choose the reading that fits.
1. The reader knows that Gregor still loves his family, but the family hears only an animal’s noise. Which kind of irony is this?
2. The family decides that a real Gregor would have left on his own, and then Gregor lovingly does. Which kind of irony is this?
3. A reader who has just finished the clerk’s speech through the door remarks that the firm has been very generous, and means the opposite. Which kind of irony is this?
4. Which Word Study word best describes Gregor’s body when the family finds it?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from Part III. Translations differ, so a word may not appear in a given edition, but each entry names the part of the story it belongs to.

lodger (noun) · a person who pays to live in a room of another’s home
Root: Old French logier (to house), from loge (hut), of Germanic origin
Where: Part III, the three renters whom the family takes in
indifferent (adjective) · without interest or concern; unmoved
Root: Latin indifferens (making no difference), from in- + differre
Where: Part III, Grete’s handling of Gregor’s food and room
emaciated (adjective) · extremely thin from hunger or illness
Root: Latin emaciare (to make lean), from e- + macies (leanness)
Where: Part III, the family’s discovery of Gregor’s body
ordeal (noun) · a severe test or painful experience
Root: Old English ordal (judgment, trial)
Where: Part III, Grete’s speech to her parents about what the family has endured
reproach (noun or verb) · an expression of disapproval or disappointment; to blame
Root: Old French reprochier
Where: Part III, Gregor’s behavior when Grete leaves the room unclean
humility (noun) · a modest or lowly view of oneself; a yielding manner
Root: Latin humilis (low), from humus (earth)
Where: Part III, the lodgers’ manner when Mr. Samsa orders them out
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM GERMAN

English took this German word for a time of shortage, when a poorer thing stands in for the real one. It came into wide English use in the twentieth century, especially for wartime substitutes.

ersatz (German: “replacement, substitute”)
The factory sold an ersatz coffee made from roasted grain, and no one at the table pretended it tasted like the real thing.
🔑 KEY TERMS
verbal irony: saying one thing and meaning the opposite.
situational irony: an outcome opposite to what was expected or fitting.
dramatic irony: the reader knows something that a character does not.
