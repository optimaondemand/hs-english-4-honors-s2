Week 3
The Lodgers
Hospitality turned inside out: who sits at the table
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Three paying lodgers take a room in the Samsa flat and dine at the family table.
The family eats in the kitchen, and the son is shut away. The lesson reads the scene as a reversal of hospitality and asks who belongs at the table.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe how the household rearranges itself around the lodgers.
2
I can explain how the scene reverses the usual meaning of hospitality.
3
I can weigh who belongs at the table and who decides.
📋 READING
▶
Reread the part of Part III from the arrival of the lodgers through their notice to leave.
📚 PART 1 · THE TABLE REARRANGED

The Samsas rent one room to three bearded gentlemen, who insist on order throughout the flat, the kitchen above all. The lodgers bring their own furnishings, so the family’s surplus goes into Gregor’s room. On the evenings when the lodgers dine in the shared living room, the door to Gregor’s room is closed.

Once, the door is left slightly open, and Gregor sees how the meal goes. The lodgers sit at the table where Gregor once ate with his parents. They unfold their napkins and look over the dishes. The mother brings meat and Grete brings potatoes. One lodger cuts into the meat in the dish to test whether it is cooked enough, and the women relax when it passes. The father enters, bows, cap in hand, and makes a round of the table before going on to the kitchen. The lodgers rise together and mutter into their beards, then eat in near silence. The family eats in the kitchen.

Gregor, who has almost stopped eating, notes with bitter surprise that the guests feed themselves well while he wastes away.

⚡ FIRST READ
A host serves guests. In this scene, who is treated as the guest, and who as the host?
📚 PART 2 · HOSPITALITY INSIDE OUT

Hospitality usually means that a host gives a place and a guest receives it. Here the guests are paying tenants, and the family defers to them in ways that show who holds power. The parents, who have never let a room before, show an exaggerated courtesy. The father does not dare sit in his own chair, and he leans against the door. When a lodger offers the mother a chair, she sits apart in a corner. When the lodgers ask that Grete play for them in the living room, the father answers for the family with the eagerness of a man who might be the player himself.

The family serves, and the guests judge. They sample each dish to judge it before they eat. They turn away from the music while the family watches their faces. When they learn that a creature has been living in the next room, one of them gives notice at once, refuses to pay for the days he has stayed, and hints at claiming damages. The other two join him.

Door closed or open a crack; full of the family's surplus. Gregor watches from the dark.
📚 PART 3 · WHO BELONGS

Belonging in this scene is bought and displayed, not given. The paying guest has a seat, and the son does not. To keep the income that holds the household together, the family shows to strangers a courtesy it no longer shows to its own member. The lodgers receive service, the family receives rent, and Gregor receives nothing, though the flat was one he chose and the household once lived on his wages.

The lodgers are indifferent to the family’s feelings, yet the family treats their reaction as decisive. Their notice becomes the occasion for Grete’s verdict on Gregor.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. What does the seating arrangement show about the household’s priorities?
2. The parents hesitate to sit in their own chairs in the lodgers’ presence. What does this show?
3. How does the scene reverse hospitality?
🔑 KEY TERMS
hospitality: the welcome and care given to a guest or stranger.
lodger: a person who pays to occupy a room in another’s home.
deference: yielding to another’s wishes or authority out of respect or need.
