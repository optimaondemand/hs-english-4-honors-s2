📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Frodo wakes in the dark, alone and afraid, and decides to stay.
Chapter 8 contains the first of the book’s tests of courage in an ordinary person. The test is not a fight he wins. It is a choice made with a way out in his pocket, and the narrator is exact about what he feels and what he does.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe how the hobbits are separated and captured on the Barrow-downs.
2
I can explain the choice Frodo faces in the barrow and what he does.
3
I can explain how the chapter treats courage in an ordinary person, including the limits of his own effort.
📋 READING
▶
Read Book I, Chapter 8, “Fog on the Barrow-downs”, in your copy.
📚 PART 1 · FOG AND THE BARROW

The day begins well. Goldberry waves the hobbits off from a hilltop, and Tom’s food and advice are in their packs. By noon they have crossed several ridges and see what they take for the line of the Road to the north. They stop in a hollow beside a tall standing stone to eat, and they doze off. They wake to find the stone cold and the sun lost in a white fog. A mist rolls up around the hollow, and they set out northward in single file with Frodo in the lead.

The fog separates them. Frodo passes between two great stones that he had not seen before, and when he looks back the others are gone. He hears cries from higher ground, climbs toward them in the dark, and comes out on a hilltop beside a barrow. A voice from the ground says it is waiting for him. A cold grip takes him, and the scene breaks off.

When he wakes, he is lying on a stone slab inside the barrow. Beside him lie Merry, Pippin and Sam, pale and dressed in white, with gold and weapons around them and a naked sword across their throats. A cold song of curses fills the room, and a long arm crawls along the floor toward Sam.

⚡ FIRST READ
How does the narrator explain the courage Frodo shows in the barrow?
📚 PART 2 · THE CHOICE

The turning point is brief and specific. The narrator pauses to say something general: There is a seed of courage hidden (often deeply, it is true) in the heart of the fattest and most timid hobbit (Book I, Chapter 8). A last, desperate danger, he adds, can make it grow. Frodo, lying on the slab, thinks of Bilbo and of the lanes of the Shire, and he finds that he is hardening toward something.

Then the arm comes closer, and an idea arrives. He thinks of putting on the Ring. If he vanished, the wight might lose him, and he might find a way out and run free over the grass, grieving for his friends but alive. He even imagines Gandalf agreeing that he could have done nothing else. The idea comes with a defence ready-made, and the defence sounds reasonable.

The narrator’s verdict is plain: the courage now awake in him outweighs the wish to flee, and he could not leave his friends so easily (Book I, Chapter 8). He wavers, fights with himself, and then seizes a short sword and hews at the arm. The hand breaks off, and the sword splinters to the hilt. A shriek follows and the light is gone.

⚖ THREE OPTIONS IN THE BARROW
OptionWhy it tempts himWhat the text shows
Put on the Ring and escapeIt would hide him, and Gandalf would admit he had no other choice.He considers it and does not do it. The narrator ties the refusal to his friends.
Strike the crawling armIt is the only act that protects Sam at once.He does it. It works only partly, and his sword breaks.
Call for helpThe sword is gone and he has no other weapon.He remembers Tom’s rhyme and sings it. The answer comes from far off.

The sequence matters. Frodo’s courage is a decision, and it does not make him strong. The sword that was supposed to protect his friends breaks, and he is saved by a rhyme he was taught the day before. The text does not treat the rescue as a reward for his effort, and it does not treat his effort as useless either. He chose, and the choice is what the chapter records.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Frodo imagines that Gandalf would agree he could have done nothing else. What does the moment show?
2. Frodo’s sword splinters when he strikes the wight’s arm. What does this do to the idea of courage in the scene?
3. Tom says that some wanderers, sons of forgotten kings, still guard the heedless. How does this connect with the Prologue?
📚 PART 3 · ORDINARY COURAGE, UNSEEN GUARDIANS

The chapter closes by widening the view. Tom gives each hobbit a dagger forged long ago by the Men of Westernesse, and he murmurs that sons of forgotten kings walking in loneliness, guarding from evil things folk that are heedless (Book I, Chapter 8) still go wandering. The hobbits see a brief vision of tall, grim men with bright swords, and the last of them wears a star on his brow. They do not understand it.

The vision echoes the Prologue’s warning about the Guardians, whose labors the hobbits forgot. Frodo has just done what a hobbit’s courage looks like in practice: a decision to stay, a blow that does not suffice, a rhyme remembered. The unseen guardians in Tom’s remark do the same on a larger scale. The book is building a picture of protection that is mostly hidden from those it protects.

🔑 KEY TERMS
barrow: a mound built over a grave.
Barrow-wight: an evil spirit that haunts the barrows and holds its captives with a spell.
temptation: a pull toward an act that seems sensible or easy and is wrong.
courage: the choice to act rightly while afraid; in this chapter it is separate from strength.
