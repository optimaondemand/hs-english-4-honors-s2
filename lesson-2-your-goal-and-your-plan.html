Your Goal and Your Plan

What a goal is here
Something your audience could do, believe, or stop doing
●
A goal is not a topic and not a theme. It is a change you want in the people you named: . And it has to be something reasonable people in your world argue about. If nobody would fight you, you are not persuading anyone.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
A magistrate of Geneva is speaking to the city council. Which of these is a goal worth a semester?
2
Write your goal
One sentence that begins with your audience
A MODEL, GENEVA WORLD
The council of Geneva should require every natural philosopher working in the city to answer, in public, for what he makes before he makes it.
Who fights it: the professors, who say knowledge dies when it has to ask permission, and the merchants, who fear the city will look backward.
Your goal. Start with your audience:
Who in your world fights this goal, and why they are not fools
Name the people and give their real reason. If you cannot find a real reason, the goal is not contested enough.

How hard will this be to win?

3
The frameworks you already know
Seven accounts of how a person flourishes, from Module 1
●
"Wise" is not a feeling. To call your goal wise you need an account of what a good human life is, and you need to show that your goal serves it. You met seven such accounts in the fall. Pick the one you will argue from. You will be held to it.
If another framework: its name and source
4
Make the case
How your goal serves a flourishing life, under the account you chose
A MODEL, GENEVA WORLD, ARGUING FROM ARISTOTLE
Aristotle says we become just by doing just acts (Book II). A philosopher who must answer for his work before he begins is practicing a habit, not obeying a rule.
The council's requirement does not punish knowledge. It trains the people who make things to think of the people their things will touch.
Your case, in a paragraph. Say what the framework holds, then show how your goal serves it.
Aim for 120 to 200 words. Use your own words for the framework, not a summary you found.
The passage you are leaning on
Book and chapter, or verse, or page. You will quote it later. For now, know where it is.
5
Steel, not straw
The best case against you, stated so well its holder would sign it
●
Anyone can knock down a weak version of the other side. Your audience will notice, and they will stop trusting you. The work you make this spring has to carry the strongest objection to your own goal, in a voice its holder would recognize as fair.
QUICK CHECK · NOT GRADED
The goal: the council should make natural philosophers answer publicly for their work. Which statement of the other side is fair?
6
Write the other side
In their words, with their reasons
The strongest case against your goal, as its best holder would state it
Write it in the first person, as if you were them. Name the good they are protecting.
Where that case comes from: a text, a framework, or a real voice in your world

Whose voice will carry this objection inside your finished work?

7
Five ways to reach people
Pick the one that reaches your audience, not the one you like most
●
A council that reads reports before it votes is reached by a written case. A crowd that gathers is reached by a speech. Readers who skim a daily paper over coffee are reached by a sharp feature. Ask what your audience already takes in, and meet them there. Click a card to choose.
What a vlog asks of you. Three episodes, 6 to 10 minutes each, scripted before you film, on camera and in your role. A page that lists every source. Your face and voice carry the argument, so the look of the frame comes from your world, not from a template.
What a podcast asks of you. Two episodes of 15 to 20 minutes, or one of 30, in role. Show notes and a sources page. Sound is your whole medium, so your music and room tone come from the world, not from a stock library.
What a feature article asks of you. One long piece, 2,500 to 3,500 words, with a headline, an opening summary, pull quotes, images you have permission to use, and sources at the end. It is published as a real web page, which Claude Code will build for you from your finished text. Claude lays it out; every word is yours.
What an imitation asks of you. You write in a real public form: testimony to a council, an op-ed series, a public lecture, or a form your world's own text uses (a dialogue, a set of sayings, letters, a speech before a council, a chronicle entry, a newspaper essay). About 3,000 words or the performed equivalent, and it is read or performed in public.
What the Address asks of you. A formal speech of 15 to 20 minutes in the classical shape: an opening, a first turn, the body of the case, a second turn, and a close. You deliver it to a gathered audience, live or recorded with the audience present, and publish the full text.

One rule about formats
Your honors medium has to differ from your regular project picks

In the regular part of this course you choose a project format each quarter from a menu: essay, debate, letters or diary, short film or screenplay, podcast or oration. Your honors medium must be a different format from what you pick for Q3 and Q4. A podcast or speech pick blocks Podcast and the Address here. A short film pick blocks Vlog. A letters or diary pick blocks a written Imitation.

9
What you do not know yet
The question your research has to answer before you can speak with authority
●
Every persuasive work rests on something the speaker had to find out. Your research question is that thing. It is not your goal restated. It is the fact, the history, or the how that you need in hand before your audience will believe you. The threads from your world card are starting points; click one to begin, then sharpen it.
A MODEL, GENEVA WORLD
Thread: Galvani, Aldini, and Davy, the real experiments behind the novel's science.
Question: What did a public lecture on animal electricity actually show an audience in the 1790s, and how far past that evidence did lecturers let the audience imagine?
Your research question
One question you cannot answer today, and could answer with sources.
10
Three kinds of sources
What you will go looking for, and where
PRIMARY SOURCES

Things made in your world by the people who lived there: a speech, a law, a letter, a record, a picture, a newspaper. You need at least three by the end. They are what your audience actually believed, in their own words.

SCHOLARLY SOURCES

Articles by scholars who studied your world. You will find them in JStor, the library database the school gives you a login for. You need three across the semester, one at a time, starting in Lesson 3.

A PERSON OR AN ARCHIVE

Someone who holds what you need: an archive, a museum, a library, a scholar, an organization. In Lesson 3 you will write to them, professionally, and ask for one specific thing.
