Week 8
What Makes a Monster?
Body, conduct, and who answers for what is made
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
The module has used the word monster in two senses: a body that frightens and conduct that harms.
The texts do not always place the two together. This lesson separates them in Gregor’s story, in Frankenstein, and in Blake’s poem, and asks who answers for the making.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can separate a monstrous body from monstrous conduct in three texts.
2
I can say who answers for the making in each case.
3
I can state a claim about what makes a monster and support it with two texts.
📋 READING
▶
No new reading. Review The Metamorphosis in your copy as needed; lines from Blake and Marcus are reprinted below.
📚 PART 1 · THE BODY AND THE CONDUCT

Gregor’s body is the one that changes. The German word for what he becomes, Ungeziefer, names a pest or vermin without fixing one species, and translators choose different English words for it. The household and the chief clerk treat the body as a reason for alarm: the chief clerk flees, the mother sinks to the floor, and the father drives Gregor back into his room with a stick.

Gregor’s conduct runs the other way. In Part I his first worry is arriving late to work. In Part II he drags a sheet over the couch so that his sister need not see him. Through the wall he follows the family’s talk and thinks of their welfare. The conduct that harms belongs to the household: the father throws apples in Part II, and one lodges in Gregor’s back and stays there. In Part III the sister begins to call him “it” and says he must go.

⚡ FIRST READ
Which character’s conduct is hardest to excuse?
📚 PART 2 · THE MAKER

The question of who made Gregor what he is begins before the change. He works as a traveling salesman to pay off his parents’ debt to his employer, and the household lives on his income while the others do little. The firm and the family have shaped him into a tool. After the change the household discovers it can earn: the father, the mother, and Grete each take work.

The other texts put the same question. In Frankenstein, Victor makes a creature and abandons it at once. The creature learns language by listening to a family and secretly leaves firewood at their door. When the family drives it away, it turns to revenge. Blake’s speaker asks the question of the maker directly:

“What immortal hand or eye
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?
...
Did He who made the lamb make thee?”
William Blake, “The Tyger,” Songs of Experience (1794; public domain)

Marcus gives a different answer to the question of how wrongdoing arises. A person acts badly “through ignorance of that which is truly good and truly bad,” and the wrongdoer is a kinsman, not a monster. On that view the household’s conduct is an error that a kinsman could correct.

📚 PART 3 · TESTING THREE DEFINITIONS

Three definitions can be tested against the cases. A: a monster is a body that frightens. B: a monster is conduct that harms another person and denies that person’s standing. C: a monster is what a community makes of a person by refusing recognition.

Definition A fits the most cases and explains the least, since it describes how others react. Definitions B and C account for the harm. Gregor is the test: his body frightens and his conduct is gentle, so the label only fits him under A. The household’s conduct fits B, and its refusal of recognition fits C. A reading to test is that in these texts monstrousness attaches more securely to conduct and to the act of making than to the body. The counter-evidence is the creature, whose violence is real, and Gregor’s body, which does alarm the people around him.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Gregor’s body frightens others, yet his conduct is considerate. What does this show about definition A, that a monster is a frightening body?
2. Marcus says a wrongdoer acts “through ignorance of that which is truly good and truly bad.” How does this bear on the module’s question?
3. Blake changes the first stanza’s “Could” to “Dare” in the last. How does this connect to who answers for what is made?
🔑 KEY TERMS
monstrous: causing fear or harm; here, a word used of bodies and of conduct.
recognition: treating a person as a person; acknowledging standing.
kinsman: a relative; for Marcus, anyone who shares reason.
creator and creature: the maker and the made, a thread from Frankenstein through Blake’s poem and Gregor’s story.
