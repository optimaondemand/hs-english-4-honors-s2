Antithesis and the Aphorism
Grammar + Vocab · Week 5
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Emerson is quotable because he builds sentences on opposites.
Antithesis is the contrast of opposed ideas in parallel form. The aphorism, a short and pointed saying, is its natural shape.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can recognize antithesis and the frame that holds it.
2
I can write a balanced sentence that sets two opposed ideas in parallel form.
3
I can distinguish a memorable statement from a supported claim.
4
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · BUILDING A BALANCED SENTENCE

Antithesis sets two opposed ideas against each other in matching grammatical form. The parallel form is the frame, and the opposition fills it. Three common frames are not X but Y, X is Y; Z is its opposite, and the reversal, A does B to C; C does B to A.

An aphorism is a short, general statement that is easy to remember. Because balance makes a line easy to remember, antithesis often carries an aphorism.

✎ ON THE BOARD · SIX BALANCED SENTENCES
It loves not realities and creators, but names and customs.
Frame: not X but Y. Realities and creators are set against names and customs in matching noun pairs.
The virtue in most request is conformity. Self-reliance is its aversion.
Two short sentences in the same form. The second names the opposite of the first.
You must court him: he does not court you.
A reversal. The verb stays the same while the subject and object trade places.
To be great is to be misunderstood.
Frame: to be A is to be B. Strictly a paradox, not an antithesis: the balanced form joins two ideas that seem opposed, an honor and a misfortune.
the only right is what is after my constitution, the only wrong what is against it.
Two parallel clauses. The second leaves out the verb, because the first has already supplied it.
Infancy conforms to nobody: all conform to it,
A reversal around a colon. Conforms to and conform to repeat while the actors swap.

Antithesis works with stories as well. Miss Brill believes she has a part; the couple on the bench sees a nuisance. The boy brings a promise to the bazaar; the bazaar brings him nothing. In each, two clauses of the same shape set a belief against what the scene shows.

📚 PART 2 · THE APHORISM AND ITS LIMITS

A balanced line can be false or unsupported. To be great is to be misunderstood reads as complete and proves nothing. The evidence is in the list of names that follows it, and the list supports only half of the sentence. An aphorism states a conclusion. It does not replace the argument for the conclusion. One well-placed antithesis can close a paragraph. A dozen in a row begin to sound glib.

Try each item, then open the answer.

Rewrite as an antithesis: Some people follow customs and other people examine what customs mean.
One answer: The conformist repeats the custom; the nonconformist tests it. The two clauses share a form, and the verbs carry the contrast.
Name the pattern in Miss Brill believes she has a part; the couple on the bench sees a nuisance.
Two clauses of the same shape (subject, verb, object) set what she believes against what the couple sees. The contrast is in the objects: a part and a nuisance.
Write an antithesis about the boy in “Araby” using the frame not X but Y.
One answer: He brought home not a gift, but a verdict on himself. The gift he promised and the judgment he found fill the same slot.
📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from the essay. Each entry gives the sense Emerson uses.

conformity (noun) · behavior that matches the group’s expectations
Root: Latin conformare (to shape together), from con- + forma (form)
The virtue in most request is conformity. (the first passage on society)
aversion (noun) · a strong dislike; the thing a person turns away from
Root: Latin aversio, from avertere (to turn away)
Self-reliance is its aversion. (the first passage on society)
suffrage (noun) · approval or support (an older sense of the word)
Root: Latin suffragium, a vote or voice of support
you shall have the suffrage of the world. (the passage on the nonconformist)
importune (verb) · to press someone with repeated requests or urgings
Root: Latin importunus (unfit, troublesome; originally “without a harbor”), from in- + portus
who was wont to importune me with the dear old doctrines of the church. (the friend’s objection)
capitulate (verb) · to give in or surrender, often on agreed terms
Root: Medieval Latin capitulare (to arrange in chapters, to settle terms), from capitulum (chapter)
how easily we capitulate to badges and names, (just after the friend’s objection)
ephemeral (adjective) · lasting a very short time
Root: Greek ephemeros (lasting a day), from epi- + hemera (day)
as if everything were titular and ephemeral but he. (just after the friend’s objection)
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN

A claim that sounds balanced can still fail to follow from what is stated. English borrowed a Latin phrase for that fault, and it applies to one common misreading of Emerson.

non sequitur (Latin: “it does not follow”)
A conclusion that does not follow from the statement before it. Example: To say that a view must be right because it is unpopular is a non sequitur, since the list of misunderstood thinkers runs from greatness to misunderstanding and not the other way.

🔑 KEY TERMS
antithesis: the contrast of opposed ideas in parallel grammatical form.
aphorism: a short, general statement that is easy to remember.
parallel structure: the repetition of the same grammatical form for related ideas.
non sequitur: a conclusion that does not follow from what comes before.
