“Hope is the thing with feathers”
Emily Dickinson on hope as a bird
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Dickinson defines hope by turning it into a small, singing bird.
The poem is one sustained comparison. Each detail of the bird makes a claim about hope: where it lives, what it sings, when it is loudest, and what it costs. Set beside Gregor’s last weeks, it supplies a counter-claim about what a self can carry when everything outside has failed.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can explain the extended metaphor and what each detail of the bird claims about hope.
2
I can explain how the three stanzas build from the bird, to the storm, to the speaker’s own witness.
3
I can compare Dickinson’s hope with Gregor’s situation and with the questioning of Herbert and Blake.

📖 THE POEM
Hope is the thing with feathers
That perches in the soul,
And sings the tune without the words,
And never stops at all,
And sweetest in the gale is heard;
And sore must be the storm
That could abash the little bird
That kept so many warm.
I ’ve heard it in the chillest land,
And on the strangest sea;
Yet, never, in extremity,
It asked a crumb of me.
Emily Dickinson, “Hope” (“Hope is the thing with feathers”), Poems, Second Series (1891; public domain)
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
Emily Dickinson (1830 to 1886) lived in Amherst, Massachusetts, and wrote nearly eighteen hundred poems. Only a handful appeared in her lifetime. The first collections were published after her death, in 1890 and 1891, and the poem on this page is in the second of them. She left her poems untitled; the editors added titles, and this one is headed “Hope.” Early editors also regularized her punctuation, so the dashes of her manuscripts do not appear in this text. The poem was written about 1861.
📚 PART 1 · THE BIRD

The first stanza states the metaphor as a definition: hope “is the thing with feathers.” The word bird does not appear until the second stanza; the first calls it only a thing with feathers.

“Hope is the thing with feathers
That perches in the soul,
And sings the tune without the words,
And never stops at all,”
Emily Dickinson, “Hope” (“Hope is the thing with feathers”), Poems, Second Series (1891; public domain)

Each detail is a claim. The bird “perches in the soul,” so hope lives inside a person, not in circumstances. It “sings the tune without the words,” so hope is wordless, more a feeling than an argument. It “never stops at all,” so hope is constant. The poem is made of such claims, and the metaphor is extended because every later detail stays inside the image of a bird.

📚 PART 2 · THE STORM

The second stanza tests the metaphor against hardship:

“And sweetest in the gale is heard;
And sore must be the storm
That could abash the little bird
That kept so many warm.”
Emily Dickinson, “Hope” (“Hope is the thing with feathers”), Poems, Second Series (1891; public domain)

The bird sings “sweetest in the gale,” so the hope the poem describes does not depend on good weather. The next lines state the claim as a challenge: it would take a very severe storm to “abash the little bird.” The bird has also “kept so many warm,” and the plural widens the claim beyond the speaker. Hope in this poem is not private property.

The poem contrasts with the two before it this week. Herbert’s speaker argues for thirty-two lines, and Blake’s speaker asks and does not receive an answer. Dickinson’s bird sings “without the words.” It makes no argument and asks no question.

📚 PART 3 · THE CRUMB

The speaker appears only in the third stanza, and as a witness, not as an actor:

“I ’ve heard it in the chillest land,
And on the strangest sea;
Yet, never, in extremity,
It asked a crumb of me.”
Emily Dickinson, “Hope” (“Hope is the thing with feathers”), Poems, Second Series (1891; public domain)

The places named are hard ones: the “chillest land” and the “strangest sea.” The last two lines turn on an ordinary fact about birds, that they come for crumbs. This one “never, in extremity” asked for any. The poem ends on the cost of hope, which is nothing.

Dickinson’s hope is not a prediction. The poem does not say the storm ends, only that the bird does not stop singing. The module question asks what makes us human, and the poem gives one answer: a capacity that persists when circumstances give no reason for it. Gregor’s last stretch tests the claim. The household’s patience runs out, and his own hope of rejoining it fades before he dies. The household also begins to count what Gregor costs, while the bird in the poem costs the speaker nothing. Whether his final warmth toward the family counts as the bird’s song is a question the story leaves to the reader.

🔑 KEY TERMS
metaphor: a comparison that says one thing is another, with no like or as.
extended metaphor: one comparison sustained across several lines or a whole poem.
abash: to daunt or embarrass.
extremity: a moment of greatest hardship.
