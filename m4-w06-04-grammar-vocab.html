Cause and Concession: Dependent Clauses
Grammar & Word Study · Week 6
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A dependent clause can give a reason or grant an objection.
Clauses that begin with because or since state cause. Clauses that begin with although, though or even though grant a fact and keep the main claim. Reading them correctly shows how an argument is built.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify clauses of cause and clauses of concession.
2
I can choose the subordinating word that fits the relation between two ideas.
3
I can punctuate a sentence with a dependent clause at the front or at the end.
4
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · REASONS AND OBJECTIONS

Both kinds of clause are adverb clauses. They cannot stand alone, and each depends on a main clause. A cause clause answers why. A concession clause names something that might seem to work against the main clause, but does not defeat it. A comma follows the clause when it comes first.

✎ ON THE BOARD · CAUSE AND CONCESSION
That is because you do not understand and cannot imagine what lies ahead
Cause. The clause introduced by because gives Elrond’s reason: the hobbits cannot picture the road. The main clause is That is.
Since the snow has closed the pass, the Company must find another road.
Cause. Since here means because, not from the time that. The clause comes first, so a comma follows it.
Although the pass is closed, the Company still has a road.
Concession. Although the pass is closed grants a fact. The main clause is the Company still has a road.
Though Aragorn distrusts the plan, he follows Gandalf’s lead.
Concession. The clause admits a doubt and the main clause shows that he acts anyway.
Because the doors open outward, no one outside can force them.
Cause. The reason comes first, so a comma separates it from the result.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from Book II, Chapters 3 to 5. Each entry gives the sense the chapters use, the root, and the chapter where the word appears.

discomfiture (noun) · defeat or embarrassment that upsets someone’s plans
Root: Old French desconfiture, from desconfire, to defeat
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 3
cordial (noun) · a restoring drink; also an adjective for warm friendliness
Root: Latin cor, heart
Where it appears: Book II, Chapters 3 and 4
sullen (adjective) · gloomy and silent in a displeased way
Root: Anglo-Norman solein, alone or solitary, from Latin solus
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 4
intricate (adjective) · having many tangled parts; hard to follow
Root: Latin intricare, to entangle
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 4
unwholesome (adjective) · harmful to health or morals
Root: un- + wholesome, from Old English hal, whole or healthy
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 4
valiant (adjective) · brave and determined
Root: Old French vaillant, from Latin valere, to be strong
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 5
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN

English uses a Latin phrase for acts or words done at the last moment, in the face of the greatest danger or at the point of death.

in extremis (Latin: “in the extremes,” at the utmost point)
At the Bridge, Gandalf acts in extremis, with the Balrog on the span and the Company behind him.
🔑 KEY TERMS
dependent clause: a clause that cannot stand alone and depends on a main clause.
cause: the reason something happens, introduced by because, since or as.
concession: a grant of a fact that might seem to work against the main claim, introduced by although or though.
