The Imperative and Self-Command
Grammar + Vocab · Week 6
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Many lines of the Meditations are commands Marcus gives himself.
The imperative mood gives a command. Its subject is usually an understood you (here, thou). A reflexive pronoun such as thyself shows that the person commanded is also the person commanding.

📚 PART 1 · MOOD AND SELF-COMMAND

A verb’s mood shows the kind of statement a sentence makes. The indicative states a fact or asks a question. The imperative gives a command or request. In an imperative the subject is nearly always an understood you: Accept what happens means [You] accept what happens. That is why an imperative can be one word long and still be a complete sentence.

Marcus writes to himself, so the understood subject is also the speaker. The reflexive pronouns thyself and myself mark this: in refresh and renew thyself, the one refreshed is the one addressed. Older English forms negative commands without do (be not) and uses let to command something other than the listener (let nothing be done).

✎ ON THE BOARD · FIVE SENTENCES FROM THE MEDITATIONS
Afford then thyself this retiring continually, and thereby refresh and renew thyself.
Imperative. The verbs afford, refresh, renew take an understood thou. Thyself points back to that same person, so Marcus commands himself.
Be not discontented, be not disheartened, be not out of hope,
Negative imperative in the older form. Modern English adds do: Do not be discontented. The verb is be; the subject is understood.
Let nothing be done rashly, and at random,
Imperative with let. The command is addressed to the writer but names a third thing, nothing, as the one to carry it out.
Accept then, and be pleased with whatsoever doth happen,
Two imperatives joined by and: accept and be pleased. The word then marks a conclusion drawn from what came before.
At what time soever thou wilt, it is in thy power to retire into thyself,
Not an imperative. This states a fact (indicative mood). Retire is an infinitive after power to, and the subject of the clause is it.

📚 PART 3 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from the Meditations passages read this week. Each entry gives the sense Marcus’s translator uses.

tranquillity (noun) · calm; freedom from disturbance
Root: Latin tranquillitas, from tranquillus (calm)
may presently afford unto him perfect ease and tranquillity.
disposition (noun) · the settled arrangement of mind and bearing
Root: Latin dispositio, from disponere (to arrange), from dis- + ponere (to place)
a decent orderly disposition and carriage
mutable (adjective) · liable to change
Root: Latin mutabilis, from mutare (to change)
things sensible be so mutable and unsettled
fallible (adjective) · capable of being mistaken
Root: Latin fallibilis, from fallere (to deceive)
and the senses so obscure, and so fallible
propitious (adjective) · favorable; well disposed
Root: Latin propitius (favorable, gracious)
either of them with a propitious and contented mind
subordinate (adjective) · placed below another in rank or order
Root: Latin subordinatus, from sub- (under) + ordinare (to order)
a thing subordinate unto the fates
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM LATIN

Marcus keeps death in view as a reminder that a life is short. English kept the Latin phrase for such a reminder.

memento mori (Latin: “remember that you must die”)
A skull painted in the corner of a portrait is a memento mori, a reminder to weigh each choice against a life that ends.
🔑 KEY TERMS
mood: the form of a verb that shows whether a sentence states, asks, or commands.
imperative: the mood that gives a command; its subject is usually an understood you.
indicative: the mood that states a fact or asks a question.
reflexive pronoun: a pronoun ending in -self that refers back to the subject.
