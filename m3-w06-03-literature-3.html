Accept What Happens
Acceptance, the physician’s prescription, and the limit of resignation
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Marcus’s hardest advice is to accept what happens, and he supports it with two images.
The images are a physician’s prescription and a rock in the sea. Four passages are read: the prescription, the promontory, the reminder that life is short, and a short rule that limits the acceptance. Together they allow a reader to weigh whether it is wisdom or resignation.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can explain Marcus’s comparison of what happens to a physician’s prescription.
2
I can describe the limit Marcus sets on acceptance.
3
I can weigh Marcus’s acceptance against Gregor’s and against Emerson’s self-trust.
📋 READING
▶
Read the passages below from Meditations (Books V, IV, VIII, and IX). Full text: Gutenberg eBook #2680.
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
The Stoics held that events follow from a rational order, so what happens to a person fits into a larger design. The translation names this order variously as “the fates,” “the nature of the universe,” and “the common nature.” The argument for acceptance depends on trust in that order, and a reader can test it without sharing the trust.
📚 PART 1 · THE PRESCRIPTION

Marcus compares what happens to a person with a physician’s orders. A physician prescribes riding to one patient, cold baths to another, and walking barefoot to a third. In the same way, he says, the nature of the universe prescribes sickness, blindness, or loss.

“As we say commonly, The physician hath prescribed unto this man, riding; unto another, cold baths; unto a third, to go barefoot: so it is alike to say, The nature of the universe hath prescribed unto this man sickness, or blindness, or some loss, or damage or some such thing. ... These therefore let us accept of in like manner, as we do those that are prescribed unto us our physicians. For them also in themselves shall We find to contain many harsh things, but we nevertheless, in hope of health, and recovery, accept of them. ... Accept then, and be pleased with whatsoever doth happen, though otherwise harsh and un-pleasing, as tending to that end, to the health and welfare of the universe”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book V

The analogy has three steps. Patients accept bitter treatment “in hope of health.” The universe is a whole whose health matters more than any part’s comfort. So a part should accept what its prescription requires. The argument holds if the whole is ordered for good. A reader who doubts that can still note what the image does: it turns hardship from an insult into an instruction.

📚 PART 2 · THE PROMONTORY AND THE SHORT LIFE

Two more passages supply the manner of the acceptance. The first is the rock in the sea. The waves beat on it continually, and it stands; around it the waves are “stilled and quieted.” The next section turns a complaint into a statement. Marcus replaces “Oh, wretched I” with “nay, happy I,” because he can continue without grief.

“Thou must be like a promontory of the sea, against which though the waves beat continually, yet it both itself stands, and about it are those swelling waves stilled and quieted. ... Oh, wretched I, to whom this mischance is happened! nay, happy I, to whom this thing being happened, I can continue without grief; neither wounded by that which is present, nor in fear of that which is to come. For as for this, it might have happened unto any man, but any man having such a thing befallen him, could not have continued without grief.”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book IV

The second passage keeps death in view. Within a short time a person is ashes or a skeleton and perhaps a name, and then “an empty sound, and a rebounding echo.” The effect is to reduce the weight of fame and of the things “dearest unto us.”

“Within a very little while, thou wilt be either ashes, or a sceletum; and a name perchance; and perchance, not so much as a name. And what is that but an empty sound, and a rebounding echo? Those things which in this life are dearest unto us, and of most account, they are in themselves but vain, putrid, contemptible. The most weighty and serious, if rightly esteemed, but as puppies, biting one another: or untoward children, now laughing and then crying. As for faith, and modesty, and justice, and truth, they long since, as one of the poets hath it, have abandoned this spacious earth, and retired themselves unto heaven.”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book V

Taken together, the passages describe acceptance as steadiness, not passivity. The rock does not flee the sea or pretend it is calm. It holds its place, and the brevity of life sets its priorities. Marcus’s own answer to what keeps a person in the world, in the passage on what is in a person’s power, is to “do good unto men.”

📚 PART 3 · WISDOM OR RESIGNATION

The acceptance has a boundary built into it. In Book VIII Marcus gives a rule, and in Book IX he gives its companion for the case where a person cannot change another person.

“Thou must therefore blame nobody, but if it be in thy power, redress what is amiss; if it be not, to what end is it to complain? For nothing should be done but to some certain end. ... Either teach them better if it be in thy power; or if it be not, remember that for this use, to bear with them patiently, was mildness and goodness granted unto thee.”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Books VIII and IX

The rule sends the reader back to the line Marcus draws between what is and is not in a person’s power. What is in a person’s power calls for action: “redress what is amiss.” What is not calls for patience. Whether the line is drawn in the right place is the question a reader has to settle, and it separates wisdom from resignation. Accepting a flood and accepting an injustice are different acts under this rule.

⚖ MARCUS’S RULE APPLIED
CaseIn someone’s power to change?What the rule asks
Illness or loss, the prescription casesno, not by the person who suffers itaccept, like the rock in the sea
A neighbor’s fault, Book IXin part: Either teach them better if it be in thy powerteach if possible; otherwise bear patiently
The Samsa household’s treatment of Gregoryes, by the household, which holds the door, the food, and the decisionaction by those who can act; acceptance does not fit

The last row is the point for this module. Gregor accepts how the household treats him: he hides, he adjusts to what the household allows, and by the end he agrees with their verdict. By Marcus’s rule the treatment lay within the household’s power, so it called for repair, not acceptance. Emerson also counsels acceptance: “Accept the place the divine providence has found for you.” Emerson’s acceptance is of one’s own time and place, and it sits beside the demand to trust oneself.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Marcus writes: “if it be in thy power, redress what is amiss; if it be not, to what end is it to complain?” What does this show about the acceptance he teaches?
2. The promontory “stands” while the waves beat on it. Which reading does the image support best?
3. By Marcus’s rule, how should the household’s treatment of Gregor be judged?
🔑 KEY TERMS
prescription: an order for treatment given by an authority, here by a physician or by nature.
promontory: a high point of land that juts into the sea.
redress: to set right what is wrong.
resignation: acceptance without the will to change what could be changed.
