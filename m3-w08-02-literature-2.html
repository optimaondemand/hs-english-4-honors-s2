Week 8
Sacrifice or Self-Destruction?
What separates a gift of the self from a loss of it
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Gregor’s end can be read as a gift to his family or as the disappearance of a self.
Marcus’s duty and Emerson’s self supply two tests: whether the act is a person’s own, and whether the self remains in it. The lesson applies the tests to the end of the story and keeps both readings open.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can state the two readings of Gregor’s end and the evidence for each.
2
I can use Marcus and Emerson to build tests that separate a gift of self from a loss of self.
3
I can apply the tests to a case and say what remains unsettled.
📋 READING
▶
No new reading. Review Part III of The Metamorphosis in your copy as needed; passages from Marcus and Emerson are reprinted below.
📚 PART 1 · THE END OF GREGOR

In Part III the household decides that Gregor must go. The sister says so openly and refers to him as “it.” Gregor retreats to his room. The story then follows his thoughts: he thinks of his family with tenderness and love, and his own conviction that he must disappear is, if anything, firmer than his sister’s. He dies as the sky outside begins to lighten. The family takes the day off and rides out into the country in the spring, and the parents notice how their daughter has grown.

Two readings follow from this. In the first, Gregor’s end is a sacrifice: out of love he gives the family what it needs, which is relief. In the second, it is erasure: he adopts the household’s verdict on his worth and consents to vanish, so the loss of a self ends in death. The text gives evidence for each, and the lesson does not choose.

📚 PART 2 · DUTY AS A GIFT

Marcus supplies the example of a duty that is a gift. The morning passage is an argument that Marcus conducts with himself, and he ends it by naming what the duty serves:

“In the morning when thou findest thyself unwilling to rise, consider with thyself presently, it is to go about a man’s work that I am stirred up. Am I then yet unwilling to go about that, for which I myself was born and brought forth into this world? Or was I made for this, to lay me down, and make much of myself in a warm bed? ... and shall actions tending to the common good of human society, seem more vile unto thee, or worthy of less respect and intention?”
Marcus Aurelius, Meditations (2nd century; Meric Casaubon’s 1634 translation; public domain), Book V

Three features mark it. Marcus decides, by argument, for a reason he can state. The act serves a good beyond himself: “the common good of human society.” And his judgment stays at work throughout. He also says that no one can make him incur anything truly shameful, since “it is not in their power to make me incur anything that is truly reproachful.”

📚 PART 3 · THE SELF AS ITS OWN AUTHORITY

Emerson supplies the other side. He calls imitation, living by copying others instead of by one’s own nature, a kind of death: a person arrives at the conviction “that imitation is suicide.” He also gives the remedy:

“Whoso would be a man must be a nonconformist. ... Nothing is at last sacred but the integrity of your own mind.”
Emerson, “Self-Reliance” (1841; public domain)

Emerson’s word suicide is figurative: it names the loss of a self that lives by borrowed models and opinions. It does not prove anything about Gregor, but it supplies a test. A self that accepts others’ verdict on its worth has lost something before it is lost in fact.

⚖ THREE TESTS
TestGift of selfLoss of self
Who decidesthe person, with the cost in view, for a reason the person can stateothers decide, or the person adopts others’ verdict on his worth
What it servesa good beyond the self that can be namednothing that can be named, or only the removal of the person
What remainsjudgment, care, and character continue through the actthe self is reduced to a function, or to absence
⚖ APPLYING THE TESTS
CaseWho decidesWhat it servesWhat remains
Marcus rising to workMarcus, by his own argumentthe common goodreason and judgment
Emerson’s nonconformistthe person, against the crowdthe integrity of his own mindthe self, intact and alone
Gregor’s endmixed: the household’s verdict, which Gregor adopts and holds more firmly than they dothe family’s relief, which he thinks of with lovehis care remains; the self ends in death

Gregor’s end passes some tests and fails others. He serves a good he names, and his care remains. He does not originate the verdict, and he adopts it. The tests do not settle the case. They show which facts a reading has to account for, and a reading of the end that ignores either his love or the household’s verdict is incomplete.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Emerson writes that “imitation is suicide.” How does that bear on Gregor’s end?
2. Marcus names “the common good” as the end of his work. What does this add to the test of whether a sacrifice is a gift?
3. Which statement fits the evidence about Gregor’s last thoughts best?
🔑 KEY TERMS
sacrifice: giving up something of value for a good beyond oneself.
erasure: removal of a person’s standing or presence; the self made to disappear.
duty: an act owed to others or to a good, as Marcus argues.
nonconformist: one who refuses a crowd’s opinion, in Emerson’s sense.
