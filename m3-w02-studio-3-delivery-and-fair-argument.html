Delivery and Fair Argument
How a pitch is spoken, and how its claims hold up when a listener pushes back.
🎯 TODAY’S FOCUS
The six-slide deck exists. This quarter it gets a voice. Session 3 covers two things: how a pitch is delivered, and how its claims stand up when a skeptical listener tests them. The Delivery Run, a recording of the full three-minute pitch, is its own graded Studio item with its own page. About 30 minutes a week goes to this work.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can control pace, pause, emphasis and presence in a three-minute delivery.
2
I can explain what kairos adds to a pitch.
3
I can find a hasty generalization, a false dilemma or an appeal to popularity in a claim and repair it.
💭 WARM-UP
A listener hears a pitch once, at the speaker’s pace. After the key number on the Problem slide, which delivery choice helps the listener most?
📖 PART 1 · DELIVERY HAS FOUR LEVERS

A deck can be sound and still fall flat when spoken. Four things are under the speaker’s control.

LeverWhat it isIn the pitch
PaceWords per minute.About 130 to 150 words a minute is a steady pace, which puts a three-minute pitch near 400 to 450 spoken words. Slow down for the claim, not for the setup.
PauseSilence on purpose.Pause before and after the number or sentence that carries the case, and before the Ask.
EmphasisOne word leaned on.Choose the one word in a sentence that carries the claim and lean on that word only.
PresenceHow the speaker occupies the room.Eyes up from the script, still hands, an upright stance. On a recording, look at the lens and keep the face in frame.

📖 PART 2 · KAIROS: WHY NOW

Kairos is the Greek word for the right moment: the point at which an argument can be heard and acted on. It differs from clock time. A claim that would be ignored at one moment can persuade at another.

A pitch uses kairos in two places. The first is the case itself: the Hook and Problem slides answer why this problem and why now, naming a change that makes the need urgent or the solution possible, and naming only a change that can be verified. The second is the room: the speaker chooses when to pause, when to move on, and when to make the Ask, after the case is built and not before.

📖 PART 3 · FAIR ARGUMENT: THREE COMMON FALLACIES

A fallacy is a flaw in reasoning that makes a claim look stronger than its support. Three turn up in pitches again and again. The sample claims below are invented for practice.

FallacyA sample claimHow to fix the claim
Hasty generalization: a small sample stretched to cover everyone“Four classmates liked the demo, so students everywhere will want it.”State the sample as it is: “Four of the five classmates who tried the demo liked it. The next test uses thirty.”
False dilemma: only two options offered when more exist“Schools either adopt this app or students keep falling behind.”Name the other options and say why this one is stronger: “Tutoring also helps. The app costs less per student and works at any hour.”
Appeal to popularity: wide use offered as proof that something works“Millions of people use study apps, so this one will work.”Replace the crowd with evidence: “In the trial, scores on the ten-question check rose from 5 to 7.”

🤖 WORKING WITH CLAUDE
Claude has one narrow job here. Paste the pitch script and ask it to list the sentences that look like a hasty generalization, a false dilemma or an appeal to popularity, or that lean on a weak claim. Ask for a list only. Claude can flag a sound claim by mistake, can miss a flaw that depends on facts about the app’s market, and can sound certain when wrong, so every item on the list gets checked against the evidence. The speaker decides whether to keep, repair or cut each claim and writes the repair in the speaker’s own words.
🧠 CHECK YOUR READ · UNGRADED
Identify the flaw an investor would hear in each claim.
1. “Five students tried the app and four improved, so it will raise scores for every school.”
2. “Students will either use this app or give up on studying.”
3. “Everyone is switching to apps like this one, so ours is the safe choice.”
🔑 KEY TERMS
kairos: the right moment for an argument; the reason a case is made now.
hasty generalization: a conclusion about many drawn from too few cases.
false dilemma: a claim that offers two options when more exist.
appeal to popularity: the use of wide agreement or wide use as proof.
