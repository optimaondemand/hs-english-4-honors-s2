The Sentence Core and Sentence Length
Grammar & Word Study · Week 5
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Every sentence, however long, rests on a core: a simple subject and its verb.
Finding the core keeps the main action in view in a long sentence. Adding material around the core controls length and emphasis. The examples come from Rivendell and the Council.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can find the simple subject and verb in a long sentence.
2
I can name the kinds of material that lengthen a sentence.
3
I can use sentence length for emphasis.
4
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · CORE AND EXPANSION

The core is the simple subject and the verb: Frodo woke. Everything else adds detail. Prepositional phrases, modifiers, appositives and clauses can all be added without moving the core. A short sentence after a long one gives the short sentence weight.

✎ ON THE BOARD · BUILDING A SENTENCE
Frodo woke.
Core: subject Frodo, verb woke.
Frodo woke in a strange bed.
A prepositional phrase adds place. The core is unchanged.
Frodo woke in a strange bed, and Gandalf, who had waited by the window, told him the date.
Two cores now: Frodo woke and Gandalf told. The clause who had waited by the window modifies Gandalf.
You have done well to come.
A short sentence. Core: You have done. Elrond’s welcome to Glóin is brief and plain.
Evil things do not come into this valley; but all the same we should not name them.
Two cores joined by a semicolon and but: things do come (negated) and we should name (negated). The length gives the comfort and the warning equal weight.
⚡ TRY IT · CORES AND LENGTH
TRY IT: find the core and judge the length.
1. In “The wounded hobbit, pale and thin after four nights of fever, slept in Elrond’s house,” what is the core?
2. Which revision of “Boromir spoke. He spoke for Gondor. Gondor had held the river.” keeps every core visible while making one sentence?
3. Which short sentence placed after a long one would give the strongest emphasis?
📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from Book II, Chapters 1 and 2. Each entry gives the sense the chapters use, the root, and the chapter where the word appears.

venerable (adjective) · worthy of deep respect because of age or dignity
Root: Latin venerari, to revere
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 1
oblivious (adjective) · unaware of what is going on around one
Root: Latin oblivio, forgetting, from oblivisci, to forget
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 1
reluctance (noun) · unwillingness; a holding back
Root: Latin reluctari, to struggle against, from re- + luctari, to struggle
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 1
heirloom (noun) · a valued object passed down in a family
Root: heir + Middle English lome, an implement or tool
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 2
grievance (noun) · a complaint based on a wrong or burden
Root: Old French grever, to burden, from Latin gravis, heavy
Where it appears: Book II, Chapter 2
dominion (noun) · rule or control over a place or people
Root: Latin dominium, from dominus, lord
Where it appears: Book II, Chapters 1 and 2
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM FRENCH

At the Council, Aragorn, heir of Isildur, describes the Rangers’ unthanked watch over simple folk. French gave English a phrase for the idea that rank carries duties.

noblesse oblige (French: “nobility obliges”)
Aragorn’s long watch over people who do not know his name is a kind of noblesse oblige.
🔑 KEY TERMS
simple subject: the main noun or pronoun that a sentence is about.
core: the simple subject with its verb.
appositive: a noun phrase placed beside another noun to rename or explain it.
