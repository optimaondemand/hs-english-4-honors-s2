Week 7
“The Collar”
George Herbert on revolt, restraint, and a call
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A speaker declares he is done with restraint, and four lines at the end change the poem.
“The Collar” is a single speech that begins in anger and is cut off by a voice. The poem’s uneven form carries the speaker’s state, and the ending raises the question of what freedom is and who belongs to whom.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can explain how the poem’s form expresses the speaker’s revolt.
2
I can trace the speaker’s argument from complaint to self-address to the call at the end.
3
I can state what “Childe” and “My Lord” establish and what the poem leaves open.

📖 THE POEM
I struck the board, and cry’d, No more;
I will abroad.
What? shall I ever sigh and pine?
My lines and life are free; free as the rode,
Loose as the winde, as large as store.
Shall I be still in suit?
Have I no harvest but a thorn
To let me bloud, and not restore
What I have lost with cordiall fruit?
Sure there was wine
Before my sighs did drie it: there was corn
Before my tears did drown it.
Is the yeare onely lost to me?
Have I no bayes to crown it?
No flowers, no garlands gay? all blasted?
All wasted?
Not so, my heart: but there is fruit,
And thou hast hands.
Recover all thy sigh-blown age
On double pleasures: leave thy cold dispute
Of what is fit, and not. Forsake thy cage,
Thy rope of sands,
Which pettie thoughts have made, and made to thee
Good cable, to enforce and draw,
And be thy law,
While thou didst wink and wouldst not see.
Away; take heed,
I will abroad.
Call in thy deaths head there: tie up thy fears.
He that forbears
To suit and serve his need,
Deserves his load.
But as I rav’d and grew more fierce and wilde
At every word,
Me thoughts I heard one calling, Childe:
And I reply’d, My Lord.
George Herbert, “The Collar,” The Temple (1633; public domain)
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
George Herbert (1593 to 1633) was an English poet and an Anglican priest, parish priest at Bemerton from 1630. The Temple, the collection that contains this poem, was published in 1633, soon after his death. The spelling here follows the old text: rode is road, winde is wind, bloud is blood, bayes is a laurel crown, deaths head is a skull, and abroad is out in the open, away from the house. The title sounds like choler, an old word for anger, and a collar is a band that restrains. Readers have also heard the priest’s calling in it.
📚 PART 1 · THE REVOLT

The poem opens in the middle of an act. The speaker has struck the table and cried “No more” and says he will go abroad. His first claim is freedom, put in similes of open space:

“I struck the board, and cry’d, No more;
I will abroad.
What? shall I ever sigh and pine?
My lines and life are free; free as the rode,
Loose as the winde, as large as store.”
George Herbert, “The Collar,” The Temple (1633; public domain)

The form is as restless as the speaker. Line lengths range from two words (“All wasted?”) to ten (“My lines and life are free; free as the rode”). Rhymes recur at uneven distances: “more” in the first line is answered by “store” in the fifth and “restore” in the eighth, and “abroad” in the second by “rode” in the fourth. The poem rhymes without settling into a pattern, and the eye sees a shape that looks out of control.

📚 PART 2 · THE ARGUMENT AGAINST RESTRAINT

The speaker’s complaint is that service has paid him badly. The harvest is a thorn that draws blood, and no restoring fruit follows. The complaint continues:

“Shall I be still in suit?
Have I no harvest but a thorn
To let me bloud, and not restore
What I have lost with cordiall fruit?
Sure there was wine
Before my sighs did drie it: there was corn
Before my tears did drown it.”
George Herbert, “The Collar,” The Temple (1633; public domain)

Even here the speaker names his own “sighs” and “tears” as what dried the wine and drowned the corn. The loss is partly self-made, and the speech turns on that point. At “Not so, my heart” he stops addressing the world and addresses himself. His heart has hands, and there is fruit. He tells it to leave its “cage” and its “rope of sands,” which his “pettie thoughts” have made.

The speaker’s argument is that the restraints are made of nothing solid, which is what a rope of sand means. Yet the same passage says these thoughts have made it “Good cable, to enforce and draw,” so the restraint, though weak in itself, has had the force of law over him. The speech ends with a maxim: the one who holds back “To suit and serve his need,” “Deserves his load.”

📚 PART 3 · THE CALL

The turn comes in the last four lines, and it is quiet:

“But as I rav’d and grew more fierce and wilde
At every word,
Me thoughts I heard one calling, Childe:
And I reply’d, My Lord.”
George Herbert, “The Collar,” The Temple (1633; public domain)

A voice calls the speaker “Childe,” and he answers “My Lord.” The speaker takes the voice to be his God, the Christian God of Herbert’s tradition, and answers as a child answers a parent. The reply is two words, set against thirty-two lines of speech. The poem does not say what the speaker does next. It says that the shouting stops and that he is named by a relation, not by a function.

The poem keeps freedom and belonging together. The restraint called a collar in the title is also the tie the last line accepts. Whether the call cancels the revolt or answers a need the revolt was covering is a question the text leaves open, and both readings have support. Gregor Samsa offers a contrast: he never revolts, and the household values him as long as he earns. The speaker revolts against service and is answered with a name.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. The speaker tells his heart to forsake “Thy rope of sands, / Which pettie thoughts have made.” What is he claiming about his restraints?
2. The speaker blames his “sighs” and “tears” for the lost wine and corn. What does this add to his case?
3. In the last four lines, what changes?
4. What does “My Lord” establish about the voice?
🔑 KEY TERMS
collar: a band that restrains; it also sounds like choler, anger.
apostrophe: address to someone or something that cannot answer, such as the speaker’s heart.
revolt: an act of refusing authority or restraint.
call: in the poem, the voice that names the speaker and ends his speech.
