💡 THE POEM

Langston Hughes (1901 to 1967) published this poem in his first book, The Weary Blues, in 1926, under the title “Epilogue”. It is now usually called “I, Too”. Its first line answers Walt Whitman’s “I Hear America Singing”.

📚 PART 1 · WHAT THE SPEAKER CLAIMS
I, too, sing America.

I am the darker brother.
They send me to eat in the kitchen
When company comes,
But I laugh,
And eat well,
And grow strong.

Tomorrow,
I’ll sit at the table
When company comes.
Nobody’ll dare
Say to me,
“Eat in the kitchen,”
Then.

Besides,
They’ll see how beautiful I am
And be ashamed,—

I, too, am America.
Langston Hughes, “Epilogue” (1926; public domain)

The speaker begins with a claim of belonging and then states the facts of his treatment: They send me to eat in the kitchen / When company comes, The word company carries the weight. He lives in the house, but guests are the ones who count as company, and when they arrive he is moved out of sight. The poem says so plainly and asks for no pity. The reply is three short actions: But I laugh, / And eat well, / And grow strong.

The tenses then change. The first stanza is in the present. The second looks ahead: Tomorrow, / I’ll sit at the table / When company comes. The same event, company arriving, will recur, but the speaker’s place will differ. Nothing in the poem asks the household for permission. The claim rests on what the speaker is, not on a favor to be granted.

The shame needs a careful reading as well. The speaker says that they will see his beauty and be ashamed, so the shame belongs to those who sent him to the kitchen, not to him. The poem closes by repeating its opening claim with one word changed. I, too, sing America. becomes I, too, am America. Singing is something the speaker does; being is something he is.

What does the speaker’s laughter in the kitchen show?
📚 PART 2 · BREE’S TABLE

Bree supplies a second case. The village welcomes the hobbits, who are strangers, and distrusts Strider, who has come and gone at the inn many times. In the common-room the Bree-folk are sympathetic to travelers from the South but not ready to take many of them in, and one of the southern travelers says, loudly, They’ve a right to live, same as other folk. The local people do not look pleased.

The hobbits are guests there, yet in the Shire they would be the ones looking down: the narrator notes that Shire hobbits call the hobbits of Bree Outsiders and think them dull and uncouth. Where a person is welcome depends partly on where the table stands.

Strider’s case is closer to the poem. He sits in a corner of the common-room, and he reports that the innkeeper would not let him in to see the hobbits. He explains the refusal without resentment: Only he does not altogether like mysterious vagabonds of my sort. Like the poem’s speaker, Strider does not argue for his place. He sits where he is put, does his work, and lets later events correct the judgment.

Who decides at Bree is hard to say. No ruler or law places Strider in the corner. Custom, rumor and the innkeeper’s comfort do it.

📚 PART 3 · A TABLE IN ANOTHER BOOK

Franz Kafka’s story, read in Quarter 3, offers a third case. In Part III the Samsa family takes in paying lodgers, who are served at the family’s own table while Gregor is kept behind a closed door. The household arranges its evenings around the guests’ comfort and treats its own son as something to be hidden.

⚖ WHO SITS WHERE
Hughes’s speakerStrider at BreeGregor in Part III
Where he is keptIn the kitchen when company comesIn a corner of the common-room, apart from the hobbitsBehind a closed door while the lodgers dine
How the group sees himAs someone who is not companyAs a rascal, a vagabond, a RangerAs a burden to be managed and hidden
Who decidesThe household that sends him awayCustom, rumor and the innkeeper’s comfortA family under strain, with its own interests at stake
What corrects the pictureHis own claim, and the shame he expects them to feelGandalf’s letter, then Strider’s conductNothing in the story while he lives

The three cases show different mechanisms. Hughes’s speaker is sent away by those who decide who counts as company. Strider is placed by custom and rumor, and is later vindicated. Gregor is placed by a family under strain and receives no later correction while he lives.

⚡ TRY IT
Choose the reading the texts best support.
1. In the poem, whose conduct does the word “ashamed” judge?
2. Which comparison between the poem and the scene in Bree holds up best?
🔑 KEY TERMS
company: guests who count, in the host’s eyes, as the people the household is for.
custom: an accepted habit of a community that settles, without any written rule, who belongs.
