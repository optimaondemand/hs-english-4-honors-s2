Alone in the Cosmos?
The self alone and the self within a whole
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
Emerson, Marcus, Herbert, and the characters of the module’s stories place the self in different relations to others.
Emerson finds the self’s authority in the self. Marcus puts the self inside a whole, and Herbert’s speaker is named by a caller. Gregor, Miss Brill, and the boy in “Araby” each look for recognition and meet its absence.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe where each text places a self’s standing.
2
I can separate solitude that is chosen from solitude that is imposed, using evidence.
3
I can state a reading of the module’s texts and test it against counter-evidence.
📋 READING
▶
No new reading. Passages from earlier weeks are reprinted below, and “Miss Brill” and “Araby” can be reread from Week 4.
📚 PART 1 · THE SELF ALONE

Gregor is alone in a room that others closed. Miss Brill is alone in a crowd. She listens each week to a band in a public garden and imagines that everyone near her is part of one company and that she has a part in the play. She hears a young couple speak about her:

“Why does she come here at all—who wants her? Why doesn’t she keep her silly old mug at home?”
Katherine Mansfield, “Miss Brill” (1922; public domain)

She goes home without her usual honey-cake, lays the fur in its box, and “when she put the lid on she thought she heard something crying.” The boy in “Araby” arrives at the bazaar too late, overhears a trivial conversation, and sees himself as “a creature driven and derided by vanity.”

Emerson treats solitude differently. For him it is where the self hears its own voice, and the world drowns it out: “These are the voices which we hear in solitude, but they grow faint and inaudible as we enter into the world.” Solitude is a source in Emerson and an injury in the stories. The difference lies in who chose it and in whether anyone answers.

⚡ FIRST READ
What do Gregor, Miss Brill, and the boy have in common?
📚 PART 2 · THE SELF WITHIN A WHOLE

Marcus answers the problem from the other side. A hand, a foot, or a head “lying by itself” is a picture of a self cut off from “the general unity,” and his remedy is the image of fellow-workers: parts of one body that work with their pairs. The self gets its standing from the whole it belongs to.

Herbert’s speaker finds standing in a different way. He rages against restraint until a voice calls him “Childe,” and he answers “My Lord.” His standing comes from a relation to a caller. Dickinson’s hope is different again: it “perches in the soul,” and requires neither a crowd nor a caller. All three describe a self that is not complete in itself, or that carries something inside it that does not depend on being seen.

📚 PART 3 · TESTING THE POSITIONS

The table sets the positions side by side. Each row names where the text places a self’s standing and what goes wrong in that text.

⚖ WHERE A SELF’S STANDING COMES FROM
TextSource of the self’s standingWhat the text shows going wrong
Emersonthe self, trusted: Trust thyselfconformity: Society everywhere is in conspiracy against the manhood of every one of its members
Marcusthe whole the self belongs to: fellow-workersa part cut off: a head lying by itself
Herberta caller who names the speaker: Childerestraint felt as a cage until the call: Forsake thy cage
Miss Brillan audience she imaginedrecognition assumed and not given: who wants her?
Gregorthe household that depended on himrecognition withdrawn: shut away, then called “it”

A reading to test is that in these texts solitude harms when recognition has been withdrawn or never given, and helps when it is chosen as a place to hear one’s own judgment. A self needs a place to be alone and someone who answers when it speaks. The counter-evidence is in Marcus, who counts cutting oneself off from the whole as a fault even when it is chosen (his inner retreat is into judgment, not away from other people), and in Emerson, who treats the crowd’s answer as the danger. The reading holds for the stories and strains against the two thinkers, which is a reason to state it with care.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Emerson says the voices heard in solitude “grow faint and inaudible as we enter into the world.” What does this say about solitude?
2. What separates Miss Brill’s belonging from the belonging in Marcus and Herbert?
3. Which statement about Gregor’s room best fits the evidence from Emerson and Marcus?
🔑 KEY TERMS
solitude: the state of being alone, whether chosen or imposed.
recognition: being acknowledged as a person by another.
belonging: standing as a member of a household, a company, or a whole.
counter-evidence: a fact that strains a reading and has to be addressed.
