“The Tyger”
William Blake on a maker and what the maker made
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
“The Tyger” is an address to an animal followed by questions about its maker.
The poem never states how the tiger was made. It asks, in a hammering rhythm, what hand and what forge could have done it, and whether the maker of the lamb made it too. The questions turn the poem toward the creator as much as the creature.

📖 THE POEM
Tiger, tiger, burning bright
In the forests of the night,
What immortal hand or eye
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?
In what distant deeps or skies
Burnt the fire of thine eyes?
On what wings dare he aspire?
What the hand dare seize the fire?
And what shoulder and what art
Could twist the sinews of thy heart?
And, when thy heart began to beat,
What dread hand and what dread feet?
What the hammer? what the chain?
In what furnace was thy brain?
What the anvil? what dread grasp
Dare its deadly terrors clasp?
When the stars threw down their spears,
And watered heaven with their tears,
Did He smile His work to see?
Did He who made the lamb make thee?
Tiger, tiger, burning bright
In the forests of the night,
What immortal hand or eye
Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?
William Blake, “The Tyger,” Songs of Experience (1794; public domain)
📚 HISTORICAL CONTEXT
William Blake (1757 to 1827) was an English poet, engraver, and painter who printed his own poems from etched copper plates with his own illustrations. “The Tyger” belongs to Songs of Experience (1794), the companion to Songs of Innocence (1789). Blake’s own spelling is “Tyger.” The printing on this page modernizes it to “Tiger.” “The Lamb,” a poem in Innocence, asks a question that “The Tyger” echoes.
📚 PART 1 · A POEM OF QUESTIONS

The poem opens with an address to the animal: Tiger, tiger, burning bright. The rest of the poem is a series of questions about its origin. No sentence states how the tiger was made, so the reader supplies no answer and receives none.

“Tiger, tiger, burning bright
In the forests of the night,
What immortal hand or eye
Could frame thy fearful symmetry?”
William Blake, “The Tyger,” Songs of Experience (1794; public domain)

The phrase “fearful symmetry” joins two opposite qualities. Symmetry is balanced proportion, a mark of design; fearful is the effect on the speaker. The poem never calls the tiger a monster. Whatever is monstrous in the scene lies in the dread the speaker feels and in the question of what kind of maker would shape such a body.

The last stanza repeats the first with one word changed. The first asks what hand or eye “Could frame thy fearful symmetry?” and the last asks what hand or eye “Dare frame thy fearful symmetry?” Could asks about ability. Dare asks about nerve, and about what the maker risked.

📚 PART 2 · THE FORGE

The middle stanzas ask how the tiger was made, and they answer in the imagery of a smith’s shop:

“And what shoulder and what art
Could twist the sinews of thy heart?
And, when thy heart began to beat,
What dread hand and what dread feet?
What the hammer? what the chain?
In what furnace was thy brain?
What the anvil? what dread grasp
Dare its deadly terrors clasp?”
William Blake, “The Tyger,” Songs of Experience (1794; public domain)

The maker appears as a craftsman working hard material: a “hammer,” a “chain,” a “furnace,” an “anvil.” Creation here is labor and force, and it carries risk, as the words “dread grasp” and “deadly terrors” show. The poem’s own hammering stress imitates the work it describes. The sequence also moves from the maker’s tools to the maker’s courage: who dares to clasp what he has made.

The fifth stanza turns from how to why. “Did He smile His work to see?” recalls the account in Genesis, in which God looks at what He has made and judges it good. The poem asks whether the maker was pleased with this work, and then asks the question that ties the poem to its companion.

“When the stars threw down their spears,
And watered heaven with their tears,
Did He smile His work to see?
Did He who made the lamb make thee?”
William Blake, “The Tyger,” Songs of Experience (1794; public domain)
📚 PART 3 · THE LAMB AND THE MAKER

“The Lamb” opens with a question of its own, and then answers it:

“Little lamb, who made thee?
Does thou know who made thee,
Gave thee life, and bid thee feed
...
He is callèd by thy name,
For He calls Himself a Lamb.
He is meek, and He is mild,
He became a little child.”
William Blake, “The Lamb,” Songs of Innocence (1789; public domain)

In “The Lamb” the maker is gentle (“He is meek, and He is mild”) and is identified with the Lamb, a name for Christ in Blake’s Christian tradition. In “The Tyger” the same kind of question has no answer. The comparison shows what the later poem asks: whether the same maker could be responsible for both the gentle creature and the terrible one.

The poem also bears on the year’s thread of creator and creature. In Frankenstein, Victor builds a being and abandons it at once, and the creature later holds him responsible for what it has become. Blake’s maker stays out of view, and the poem puts the question to the thing made and to the reader. Both texts ask who answers for a made thing. In The Metamorphosis the question appears from another side: the firm and the household shaped Gregor into a tool before the change, and the family decides what to do with him after it.

🔑 KEY TERMS
symmetry: balanced proportion; a sign of design.
apostrophe: address to someone or something that cannot answer, such as the tiger.
maker: the one who shapes a thing; in the poem, an unseen figure asked about but never described.
Innocence and Experience: the two parts of Blake’s Songs; the lamb belongs to the first and the tiger to the second.
