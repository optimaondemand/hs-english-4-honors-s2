The Music
A violin, a lowest point, and the question Gregor puts to himself
📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
In his worst state, Gregor hears his sister play the violin and is drawn out of his room.
The scene tests whether being moved by beauty marks a person as human. The lesson reads Gregor’s condition at the start of Part III and then the evening of the music.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can describe Gregor’s condition and routine at the start of Part III.
2
I can explain what Gregor’s response to the music suggests about what remains human in him.
3
I can explain the irony that the longing that draws him out also exposes him.

📚 PART 1 · THE LOWEST POINT

Part III opens with the apple still lodged in Gregor’s back, a wound no one dares to remove, and his movement much reduced. Each evening the living-room door is opened, and from the dark he watches the family in the lamplight. They are quieter than before. The father dozes in his uniform, which he has stopped taking off even at home. The mother sews for a fashion shop. Grete, now a shop assistant, studies shorthand and French at night.

The household has dismissed the maid, hired a charwoman for the rough work, and sold some of the family jewelry. Grete now pushes food into Gregor’s room with her foot on her way out and sweeps it away later, caring little whether it was eaten. Gregor has almost stopped eating. His room has become a store for whatever the family has no space for, including kitchen refuse. The charwoman looks in on him with a rough familiarity.

⚡ FIRST READ
Before the violin, what has Part III shown about Gregor’s standing in the household?
📚 PART 2 · THE MUSIC

One evening three lodgers, who rent a room in the flat, are in the living room after their meal when the sound of a violin reaches them from the kitchen. They ask that Grete play for them in the room, and the family brings out the stand and the music. The lodgers soon lose interest and withdraw to the window, smoking, with the air of men too polite to leave.

Gregor has not heard Grete play in all this time. He is drawn forward until his head is in the living room, careless of the dust and scraps he carries on his body. He asks himself whether a mere animal could be affected so deeply by music. The narration answers in an image: it seems to him that a way is opening toward a food he has wanted and never named. He imagines taking Grete into his room and keeping her there by her own free will. He pictures telling her the plan he had kept secret: a place for her at the conservatory.

Appetite explains why Gregor eats. It does not explain why he leans toward a violin. The scene gives the story’s strongest evidence that a human sensibility survives in a body that is no longer human. It also marks a change in him: the narration notes that he once took pride in his consideration for others and now hardly thinks of it.

📚 PART 3 · THE LONGING THAT EXPOSES HIM

The middle lodger points at Gregor and calls out to Mr. Samsa. What follows is the next lesson’s subject. The irony is already in place: the feeling that most shows Gregor’s humanity is the one that gets him seen. The family, absorbed in the music, had not noticed him. The lodgers, bored by it, did.

⚡ TRY IT · WHAT DOES THE EVIDENCE SHOW?
Choose the reading the text best supports.
1. Gregor asks whether an animal could be so moved by music. What does the question imply?
2. How do the lodgers’ reaction to the music and Gregor’s differ?
3. What is ironic in the way Gregor is discovered?
🔑 KEY TERMS
sensibility: the capacity to respond to beauty and feeling.
irony: a gap between what is expected or fitting and what happens.
