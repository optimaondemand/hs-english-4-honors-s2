📖 TODAY’S FOCUS
A story’s point of view decides who can be known from the inside.
The Metamorphosis stays close to Gregor. A technique called free indirect discourse lets his thoughts appear in the narration with no tag such as “he thought” and no quotation marks. The lesson names the technique, and Word Study takes six words from Part I.
🎯 OBJECTIVES
1
I can identify third-person limited point of view and explain its effect.
2

3
I can define and use this week’s Word Study words.
📚 PART 1 · POINT OF VIEW AND THE THOUGHT ON THE PAGE

Point of view is the position from which a story is told. Third-person limited uses he or she but stays inside one character’s perceptions, so the reader knows what that character sees and thinks and learns about everyone else from outside. The Metamorphosis is told this way, anchored to Gregor.

Three ways to report a thought
Direct: the thought is quoted and tagged (he told himself, “...”).
Indirect: the thought is reported with that and shifts to the narrator’s grammar.
Free indirect: the narrator’s voice carries the thought itself, with no tag and no quotation marks.
✎ ON THE BOARD · ONE THOUGHT, THREE WAYS
Gregor told himself, “The clerk will understand.”
Direct. The tag and the quotation marks mark the thought as Gregor’s.
Gregor told himself that the clerk would understand.
Indirect. The tag remains, but the words are the narrator’s.
Surely the clerk would understand. Five years without a sick day had to count for something.
Free indirect. No tag, no quotation marks; the thought reads as narration and as Gregor’s mind at once.
The clerk’s hand rose to his mouth, and Gregor could not tell whether it meant fear or contempt.
Third-person limited. The clerk’s face is seen from outside, and only Gregor’s reaction is known from inside.

In Part I the technique does a specific job. Because Gregor’s thoughts arrive without a tag, his worries about trains and bosses reach the reader as plain fact. The reader takes in his priorities before judging them, which is why the misplaced worry registers as an observation about his life and not as a joke.

📚 PART 2 · WORD STUDY

These six words come from Part I. Translations differ, so a word may not appear in a given edition, but each entry names the part of the story it belongs to.

vermin (noun) · small creatures regarded as pests; also a contemptuous word for people
Root: Latin vermis (worm), through Old French vermine
Where: Part I, the opening paragraph, where the narration names what Gregor has become (the word varies by translation)
strenuous (adjective) · requiring great effort
Root: Latin strenuus (vigorous, active)
Where: Part I, Gregor’s complaints about the traveling career
subordinate (noun) · a person who works under another’s authority
Root: Latin sub (under) + ordinare (to arrange), from ordo (order)
Where: Part I, Gregor’s thoughts about the boss’s desk and how he speaks down to his staff
suspicious (adjective) · inclined to distrust; showing distrust
Root: Latin suspicere (to look up at, to mistrust), from sub + specere (to look)
Where: Part I, Gregor’s thoughts about the firm sending the chief clerk to the flat
diligence (noun) · steady, careful effort
Root: Latin diligentia, from diligere (to value, to choose)
Where: Part I, Gregor’s speech to the chief clerk through the door
retreat (verb) · to move back; to withdraw
Root: Latin retrahere (to draw back), through Old French
Where: Part I, the chief clerk backing away when the door opens
🌐 LOANWORD SPOTLIGHT · BORROWED FROM GERMAN

Kafka wrote in German, and English has borrowed several German words for states of mind. This one describes a general, deep anxiety with no single clear cause.

angst (German: “anxiety, fear”)
A quiet angst settled over the house whenever the post arrived, though no one could say what they feared.
🔑 KEY TERMS
point of view: the position from which a story is told.
third-person limited: narration in he or she that stays inside one character’s perceptions.
free indirect discourse: a character’s thought carried in the narrator’s voice, with no tag and no quotation marks.
